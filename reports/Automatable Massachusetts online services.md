# Turn 351 Town Agendas Into Subscription Revenue

The best software-only opportunity in this research is a **Massachusetts clean-energy and land-use meeting monitor**: software that reads the agendas every town board must post under the Open Meeting Law, flags items on battery storage, solar, moratoria, special permits, 40B housing and cell towers, and sells keyword alerts by subscription to developers, land-use attorneys and consultants. The model already makes money elsewhere: Hamlet monitors **more than 3,000 U.S. governing bodies** and has reportedly raised about $10M, OrdinanceWatch sells quote-priced alerts to Florida and Georgia law firms and associations, and Britain's Open Council Network lists business and API plans from **£50 to £549 a month** ([PublicCEO](https://www.publicceo.com/2026/02/hamlet-launches-nationwide-public-meeting-coverage-over-3000-local-governments-videos-now-discoverable/); [Signalbase](https://www.trysignalbase.com/news/funding/hamlet-secures-5m-seed-investment-for-ai-powered-public-meeting-insights); [OrdinanceWatch](https://ordinancewatch.com/); [Open Council Network](https://opencouncil.network/api/pricing)). Massachusetts looks underserved because its 351 towns each publish on their own websites with no statewide aggregator found, and because since **October 1, 2026** every town must offer a 12-month consolidated permit for small clean-energy and storage projects while several towns pursue battery-storage moratoria ([ABA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/environment_energy_resources/resources/natural-resources-environment/2026-spring/ray-hope-massachusetts-renewables-permitting-reform); [Lee, MA](https://leema.gov/DocumentCenter/View/4860/Planning-Board-Public-Hearing-Bess-Moratorium-Bylaw-Amendment-April-27)). At an estimated $79–$299 a month per business, it needs dozens of customers rather than thousands, which matters when the typical independent software product earns a median of roughly **$170–$330 a month** ([BigIdeasDB](https://bigideasdb.com/state-of-indie-saas-revenue-2026); [Quasa on TrustMRR](https://quasa.io/media/startup-reality-the-hard-numbers-from-trustmrr)). The second product should be **Construction Supervisor License (CSL) exam prep**, where state board minutes record a pass rate near **25%** after the 2025 switch to a new test vendor (PSI) and the 10th-edition building code, and a local school still charges $249 ([BBRS minutes](https://www.mass.gov/doc/bbrs-minutes-4-8-25/download); [At Leisure](https://atleisurelicense.com/node/27556)). My estimates, not sourced figures, put the monitor at roughly $8,000–$95,000 a year in recurring revenue after one to two years and exam prep at $12,000–$54,000 a year, with a real chance of much less. Claude can build both and run the crawling, classification, alerts, content updates and billing email on a schedule, while the owner must register the business, pass identity checks at Stripe, the bank and Google, approve spending, pay for a one-time legal review, hold every root login and answer legal notices. Spend the first two weeks checking whether Hamlet or the new SitePath platform already covers Massachusetts planning and zoning boards; if they do, launch exam prep first.

## Seven tests cut forty ideas to two worth building

The research examined more than forty ideas (my tally across six research threads) against the user's constraints of no physical work, no licensed staff, no routine per-customer case work and no sales calls. Each survivor was then scored on seven tests: proof that customers pay for it in other U.S. markets, evidence of a Massachusetts gap, competition including free and AI-written alternatives, legal exposure, whether customers can find it without sales calls, seasonality and time to first revenue, and how fully software can deliver it. The table ranks the nine that passed first screening; the ratings are my judgments from the evidence cited below.

One pattern explains most of the order: **if the product is a document a chatbot can write on request, it is being commoditized; if its value is a dataset that must be kept current, it is not.** The WISP generator shows the first problem. A WISP is a written information security program, and its legal driver is broad, since 201 CMR 17.00 requires every holder of Massachusetts residents' personal information to keep one. Paid versions sell for **$249–$499 one-time** (Verito), and auto-updating policy documents sell for **$119 a year** (Termageddon) ([201 CMR 17.00](https://www.mass.gov/doc/201-cmr-1700-standards-for-the-protection-of-personal-information-of-residents-of-the-0/download); [Verito](https://www.verito.com/buy-written-information-security-plan); [Termageddon](https://termageddon.com/pricing/)). Yet the state publishes a free compliance checklist, the IRS offers a free 28-page template, Boston IT firms give templates away, and any general-purpose AI model drafts one for nothing, which leaves paid value mainly in record-keeping that most small businesses don't know they need ([OCABR checklist](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/201-cmr-1700-compliance-checklist); [CPA Practice Advisor](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2025/07/29/irs-reminds-tax-pros-of-requirement-for-a-written-security-plan-wisp/165822/); [PowerUp Boston](https://powerupboston.com/resources/wisp-template)).

The property-tax abatement packet has the best-proven model of all: Ownwell has processed more than **1 million appeals** and raised a $50M Series B in February 2026 ([VCA Online](https://www.vcaonline.com/news/2026021908/ownwell-raises-50m-launches-national-service-to-streamline-property-tax-appeals-and-make-home-ownership-more-affordable/)). But Massachusetts is not empty. AppealDesk already sells a **$49 do-it-yourself packet and says it covers all 351 Massachusetts towns**, Ownwell launched a national packet for states outside its core markets, and Boston's filing window runs only from late December to February 1 ([AppealDesk](https://www.appealdesk.com/compare/best-massachusetts-property-tax-appeal-services); [boston.gov](https://www.boston.gov/departments/assessing/how-file-real-estate-tax-abatement)). A site launched in December would be selling a $49 product with no search ranking into a six-week window.

The landlord deposit kit ranks third because the pain is real and specific to Massachusetts. A landlord who mishandles a security deposit can owe **three times the deposit plus attorney's fees**, and within 30 days the tenant must get a receipt naming the Massachusetts bank and the account number ([c. 186 §15B](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartII/TitleI/Chapter186/Section15B)); TurboTenant's Massachusetts receipt page leaves those bank details out ([TurboTenant](https://www.turbotenant.com/rent-collection/rent-receipt/massachusetts/)). Its weaknesses are unmeasured demand and a consumer-sized ticket.

The rest fall away for different reasons. Kids' activity alerts have proven pricing (Campnab charges $10–$30 a month) and vivid local demand, with the state's DCR warning that most Learn to Swim applications will be rejected, but they need thousands of parents and scrapers spread across many town recreation platforms that break often ([Campnab](https://campnab.com/pricing); [DCR](https://app.smartsheet.com/b/form/8ccac316ef164e4b97e2b99d16886886)). The building-permit feed has the most impressive comparable in Shovels, at an estimated **$3M in annual recurring revenue** charging $599–$999 a month, but its Massachusetts gap is unverified and Construction Monitor already appears to supply permits for Boston, Cambridge and Framingham ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/shovels/); [Levelset](https://levelset.com/contractors/elaine-construction/permits)). Public-bid alerts compete with free COMMBUYS search and BidNet's free tier ([OIG Chapter 30B Manual](https://maoig.gov/wp-content/uploads/Chapter-30B-Manual-2026.pdf); [Civic IQ](https://civiciq.com/blog/civic-iq-bidnet-direct-pricing)). Early-distress lists built from Land Court mortgage-default (servicemember) and tax-lien filings work technically, but customers would contact defaulted homeowners, some of them military, and the state court's portal terms ban scraping and resale, so the solicitation and court-data risk lands on the owner ([Land Court reports](https://www.mass.gov/lists/land-court-masscourts-reports); [Attorney Portal terms](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/attorney-portal-terms-of-use)).

Several ideas fail the constraints outright. Continuing-education courses look automatable, but every Massachusetts licensing board requires a credentialed person on record: a CSL course provider needs a registered course coordinator ($400) and instructors with years of field experience, and the rule text found caps online CSL credit at 6 of the 12 required hours ([780 CMR 110.R5.4](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/massachusetts/780-CMR-110-R5-4)). That is licensed staff by another name. Heating-oil price comparison, street-sweeping alerts and docket alerts are already served in Massachusetts, often free ([HeatFleet](https://heatfleet.com/); [Cambridge Day](https://www.cambridgeday.com/2024/12/17/service-helps-its-users-prevent-a-ticket-or-a-tow-keeping-drivers-from-spaces-too-good-to-be-true/); [Social Law Library](https://www.socialaw.com/about/news-and-noteworthy/2026/august-2026/trellis-search-state-court-dockets/)). Homestead-declaration help carries county registry warnings about companies charging for a free form, and short-term-rental tax help is moot because Airbnb remits Massachusetts occupancy taxes for hosts ([Salem Deeds](https://www.salemdeeds.com/SalemDeeds/DownLoadFile.aspx?newsid=120); [Airbnb Help](https://www.airbnb.com/help/article/2587)).

## The October 1 permitting rule turns town agendas into a paid signal

Paid public-meeting monitoring is an established, venture-funded category. Hamlet, founded in 2022, said in February 2026 that it monitors more than 3,000 councils, planning commissions and other bodies nationwide; it sells custom-priced software to real-estate developers and city governments, offers a 14-day free trial with keyword alerts, and uses human fact-checkers on its AI summaries ([PublicCEO](https://www.publicceo.com/2026/02/hamlet-launches-nationwide-public-meeting-coverage-over-3000-local-governments-videos-now-discoverable/); [Signalbase](https://www.trysignalbase.com/news/funding/hamlet-secures-5m-seed-investment-for-ai-powered-public-meeting-insights)). OrdinanceWatch sells advance-notice alerts in 128 issue categories to associations and to law firms that use its top plan as a "client retention tool," but covers only Florida and Georgia ([OrdinanceWatch](https://ordinancewatch.com/)). SitePath Intelligence launched in August 2026 to sell permitting, zoning, moratorium and planning-decision intelligence specifically to energy developers ([Utility Dive](https://www.utilitydive.com/press-release/20260807-sitepath-intelligence-launches-county-level-permitting-intelligence-platfor-1/)). Few vendors publish prices, so the clearest anchors are Open Council Network's £50-a-month business plan and £249–£549 API plans, plus press associations in Colorado and Wisconsin that charge about **$75 a month** for public-notice keyword alerts ([Open Council Network](https://opencouncil.network/about/business); [Colorado Smart Search](https://www.publicnoticecolorado.com/SmartSearchSignup.aspx); [WisBlawg](https://wisblawg.law.wisc.edu/?p=2191)).

The Massachusetts trigger is new and dated. The 2024 Climate Act left towns in charge of generation under 25 MW and storage under 100 MWh and created a consolidated local permit with a 12-month decision clock, under which the **permit is approved by default if a town misses the deadline**. The rule, 225 CMR 29.00, was issued February 27, 2026, and towns had to offer the pathway by **October 1, 2026** ([ABA](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/environment_energy_resources/resources/natural-resources-environment/2026-spring/ray-hope-massachusetts-renewables-permitting-reform); [DOER slides](https://www.mass.gov/doc/doer-clean-energy-siting-permitting-public-information-session-presentation-slides/download)). Towns are improvising: Sutton began accepting consolidated applications on October 1 while still choosing between its own bylaw and the state energy office's model rules ([Sutton](https://suttonma.gov/m/newsflash?cat=7)). Meanwhile Lee and Sturbridge held 2026 hearings on battery-storage moratoria, and Becket and Worthington asked the Legislature for moratoria on large solar and storage ([Sturbridge](https://www.sturbridge.gov/node/171911); [H.5641](https://malegislature.gov/Bills/194/H5641); [H.5294](https://malegislature.gov/Bills/194/H5294)). A developer with sites in a dozen towns now has to watch planning boards, zoning boards, conservation commissions and town-meeting warrants at once, because one moratorium article or bylaw vote can kill a project.

The data is public by law but scattered. Every local board must post notice at least 48 hours before a meeting, excluding weekends and holidays, listing the topics the chair expects to discuss ([AG notice checklist](https://www.mass.gov/doc/11062024-oml-notice-checklist/download)). Each town posts its own way: Westford, Middleborough and Middleton run "Notify Me" sign-up lists, with Middleton requiring a separate sign-in per board; West Boylston, Hanover, Wayland and Norwell post agenda PDFs on what looks like a shared Granicus-hosted platform; and Holbrook handles Planning Board agenda requests by phone or email ([Middleton](https://middletonma.gov/list.aspx); [West Boylston](https://www.westboylston-ma.gov/sites/g/files/vyhlif1421/f/agendas/march_15_2024_planning_board_agenda_for_posting.pdf); [Hanover](https://www.hanover-ma.gov/sites/g/files/vyhlif12081/f/agendas/planning_board_agenda_8-19-24.pdf); [Holbrook](https://www.holbrookma.gov/print/34)). I estimate Massachusetts has roughly 2,000–4,000 relevant boards, about as many as Hamlet covers nationwide, so national tools probably index only part of the state's small-town boards. That remains unverified, because Hamlet's coverage list and pricing page could not be retrieved. The state newspaper association's legal-notice alerts are free during an introductory period, but they cover published hearing ads, not agendas, warrants or minutes ([MA Smart Search](https://www.masspublicnotices.org/SmartSearchSignup.aspx)).

**How it works and makes money.** The product is a self-serve web app: a customer picks towns and topics and receives instant alerts plus a weekly digest, each item linked to its source PDF with the board, hearing date, address and applicant extracted. Revenue comes from Stripe subscriptions. My starting price hypothesis, anchored to the comparables above, is a **$79-a-month town pack** (about 15 towns, one user), a **$299-a-month statewide plan** (all towns, three users, spreadsheet export), annual prepay at two months free, and a quoted data or API license later. A 14-day trial without a card fits typical self-serve software rates of about 8.5% of visitors starting a trial and about 18% of trials converting ([Powered by Search](https://www.poweredbysearch.com/learn/b2b-saas-trial-conversion-rate-benchmarks/)). Free public pages carry the marketing: a statewide battery-storage and solar moratorium tracker, and a map of which towns adopted the state's model permit rules versus their own bylaws. Those pages contain combined data that exists nowhere else, which is the kind of content Google's spam rules allow.

| Scenario (my estimates) | Subscribers | Annual recurring revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 8 town packs | about $7,600 |
| Middle | 20 town packs + 5 statewide plans | about $37,000 |
| High | 45 town packs + 15 statewide plans | about $96,000 |

Three to eleven town-pack subscribers would cover the estimated monthly operating costs of $180–$850, so even the low case beats the indie median; the middle case is a good outcome after 12–24 months, not a floor.

**Risks and the kill test.** The decisive unknown is overlap: if Hamlet already indexes most Massachusetts zoning boards and conservation commissions with self-serve alerts, the gap shrinks to price and depth. Accuracy matters too, as Hamlet's human fact-checkers suggest, since a missed hearing costs a customer real money; the product should never state a fact it cannot link to a source, should publish a dashboard showing every monitored board and its last successful crawl, and should disclaim completeness. Business buyers paying $299 a month sometimes expect a call, and published prices, a recorded walkthrough and the trial substitute only partly, so the owner should expect email questions. The number of Massachusetts solar and storage developers, land agents and land-use attorneys is also unmeasured. The validation test takes two weeks: Claude samples 30 towns' planning boards, zoning boards and conservation commissions in Hamlet's free search, checks SitePath's stated coverage, and ships the free moratorium tracker with a waitlist. Proceed if national tools miss most sampled boards and roughly 20 business emails join within 30 days; those thresholds are my suggestion, not research findings.

## A 25% pass rate makes contractor exam prep the second product

Massachusetts has no general-contractor license; the relevant credential is the CSL, regulated by the Board of Building Regulations and Standards (BBRS) and tested by PSI ([OpenExamPrep](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/ma-general-contractor/introduction/exam-facts)). After the January 2025 move from Prometric to PSI and to the 10th-edition code, the board's April 8, 2025 minutes recorded about **1,100 exams and 650 first-time takers since January**, a pass rate near **25% against 51% under Prometric**, and candidates retaking two to four times, sometimes within two weeks ([BBRS minutes, April 2025](https://www.mass.gov/doc/bbrs-minutes-4-8-25/download)). By September 2025 the head of the state's Division of Occupational Licensure was meeting PSI monthly to review questions passing below 60% ([BBRS minutes, September 2025](https://www.mass.gov/doc/bbrs-minutes-9-9-25/download)). Annualizing the early-2025 figures gives roughly 2,400 first-time candidates and 4,000 attempts a year, my estimate from a single data point that may include pent-up demand from the vendor switch.

People already pay for this, and the incumbents are dated. At Leisure sells 10th-edition unrestricted prep for **$249**, still lists a 9th-edition version at the same price, and keeps an explainer citing 2015 and 2018 codes that predate the 2021 national codes behind the current state code ([At Leisure 9th edition](https://atleisurelicense.com/node/11301); [At Leisure explainer](https://atleisurelicense.com/node/17371)). Massachusetts Construction School is listed at $147–$567 ([PocketSuite](https://directory.pocketsuite.io/?p=3681)). Florida contractor prep runs $149–$999 in a competitor-published comparison, and 1 Exam Prep's Florida business-and-finance course sells for about $300 ([EstatePass](https://www.estatepass.ai/contractor/best-contractor-exam-prep/); [Zack Academy](https://www.zackacademy.com/corporate-training/contractor-licensing-and-renewal/fl-business-and-finance-cbt-prep---online)).

The main threat is the same kind of business the user would build. OpenExamPrep publishes a free 10th-edition CSL guide updated July 2026 and earns money from AI credits and affiliate links, and EstatePass offers more than 2,000 free contractor questions with a paid tier from $9.99 a month ([OpenExamPrep](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/ma-general-contractor/introduction/exam-facts); [EstatePass](https://www.estatepass.ai/)). A paid product has to win on what free text can't easily match: every answer checked against the code section it cites, timed mock exams in PSI's format, open-book "find it fast" lookup drills (candidates must bring the IBC, IRC, IECC, 780 CMR, 521 CMR, 225 CMR and OSHA 1926), guides to tabbing the code books, and separate restricted, unrestricted and specialty tracks. Whether that beats free remains untested.

Copyright sets the build rules. Courts have held that privately written standards keep their copyright after being adopted into law, and the International Code Council lets the public read its codes free online but charges for copying ([ICC Building Safety Journal](https://www.iccsafe.org/building-safety-journal/bsj-hits/federal-court-rules-standards-do-not-lose-copyright-protection/); [Construction Dive](https://constructiondive.com/news/icc-v-upcodes-can-a-private-organization-copyright-the-law/558723)). Questions must cite section numbers and paraphrase rather than reproduce code text. Massachusetts-written amendments are probably quotable under the Supreme Court's government-edicts ruling, but that is an inference for a lawyer to confirm ([ICC on Georgia v. PRO](https://www.iccsafe.org/building-safety-journal/bsj-hits/supreme-court-decision-bolsters-the-code-councils-copyright-case/)). Real or remembered PSI exam items are off limits.

**How it makes money.** A one-time $99–$149 payment buys six months of access, and Amazon affiliate commissions on the required code books add a second stream, the same model OpenExamPrep uses. Selling to 5–15% of about 2,400 first-time candidates would bring in about **$12,000–$54,000 a year** (estimate); the research notes' 30% case reaches about $70,000, which I treat as a ceiling. Online access to a prep tool is likely taxable software in Massachusetts at 6.25%, while a downloadable e-book may not be, a question worth one accountant's consultation ([Kintsugi](https://trykintsugi.com/blog/massachusetts-saas-sales-tax); [Numeral](https://www.numeral.com/blog/saas-sales-tax-massachusetts)).

The risks are concrete. Demand could fade if PSI fixes its questions, and the volume data is 18 months old. AI-written questions can be wrong, as free hoisting-license study PDFs that contradict the state's own license classes show ([mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/guide-to-engineering-restrictions)), so verifying answers likely requires the owner to buy the code books, whose cost was not researched. The FTC's DoNotPay order shows unproven performance claims are punishable, so no pass-rate marketing without data ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/87474)). Exam prep is still the right second product because it diversifies the business: one-time consumer sales, no scraping, almost no upkeep until the next code edition, and a launch within weeks. If the owner prefers recurring consumer revenue, the landlord deposit kit is the alternate.

Stripe requires a U.S. individual's Social Security number for a sole proprietor's representative, and Google Ads can require identity verification before reviewing a suspension appeal ([Stripe](https://support.stripe.com/questions/business-rep-owner-tax-id-requirements-for-us-companies-sole-proprietorship-accounts); [Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/9703665)). ### Three dates set the launch calendar

October 1, 2026 has already passed, so the window that matters now is the spring 2027 town-meeting season, when moratorium and permit-bylaw articles go to votes; in my estimation the monitor should cover its core towns by about February 2027 to catch the warrants. Lee's proposed moratorium would run until November 14, 2026 or until zoning is adopted, which makes it an early test case. **February 1, 2027 is Boston's abatement deadline**, with filing opening in late December; selling into this season would mean launching by early December with no search ranking, so I recommend skipping it. CSL prep has no fixed deadline, but each month PSI keeps revising low-scoring questions the pass-rate pain may ease, so ship within about 60 days. The state privacy bill has sat in a conference committee since June 2026, so check its status before adding any product that sells personal data ([malegislature.gov](https://malegislature.gov/Bills/194/S2619/BillHistory)). A sensible 90-day sequence runs validation in weeks one and two while the owner registers the business and Stripe account; has Claude build the monitor for towns on the two most common website platforms, and the exam question bank in parallel, in weeks three through eight; and opens paid plans, small ad tests and owner-approved outreach in weeks nine through twelve.

## Typical outcomes, slow search and thin data cap the forecast

Typical outcomes are small. The 2025 group in a dataset of 3,787 self-funded software products had a **median of $168 a month**, and only 23.5% topped $1,000 a month ([BigIdeasDB](https://bigideasdb.com/state-of-indie-saas-revenue-2026)). TrustMRR, which verifies revenue through payment providers, showed a $334 median, and among AI startups under $5,000 a month the median was $177 against a $1,539 average, so a few big earners skew the picture ([Quasa](https://quasa.io/media/startup-reality-the-hard-numbers-from-trustmrr)). Products that reached $1,000 a month took a median of eight months in a small payment-verified sample, and a rough estimate puts the share of attempts reaching $10,000 a month near 3.5% ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/it-takes-5-months-to-reach-1k-in-mrr-491742f806); [softwaredesign.ing](https://www.softwaredesign.ing/blog/real-world-stats-for-bootstrapping)). The headline "AI-run company" stories mostly report numbers no one has audited.

Finding customers is the hard part. Only **1.74% of newly published pages reach Google's top 10 within a year**, and the average #1 result is five years old ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-rank/)). Google's spam policy, last updated August 28, 2026, names "using generative AI tools… to generate many pages without adding value" as abuse, so the plan publishes data pages only, never 351 AI-written town guides ([Google Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies)). Search ads average about **$5.26–$5.42 per click**, with legal terms near $8.58 ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/google-ads-costs-keep-rising-but-conversion-rates-improved-in-2025-477927); [Digital Gravity](https://www.digitalgravity.ae/blog/?p=5305)); at the trial-conversion rates above, that implies roughly $330 to win one customer (my arithmetic), which is affordable for a $79–$299 subscription, thin for $99–$149 exam prep and prohibitive for a $49 abatement packet. Outreach email must meet Gmail's authentication rules and stay below a 0.3% spam-complaint rate, federal CAN-SPAM penalties run up to **$53,088 per violation**, and marketing texts still require prior written consent, so SMS is off the table ([Google](https://support.google.com/a/answer/81126); [FTC](https://search.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/ftc-publishes-inflation-adjusted-civil-penalty-amounts-2025); [Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/eleventh-circuit-vacates-tcpa-11-consent-rule)). More than 5,000 website-accessibility lawsuits were filed in 2025, which is why accessibility is built in from the start ([UsableNet via Newswire](https://www.newswire.com/news/digital-accessibility-lawsuits-surge-in-2025-22618502)).

The research has real holes. The web-search budget ran out in every research thread, and mass.gov blocked automated page fetches, so many state facts come from search excerpts rather than full documents. Still unknown are Hamlet's Massachusetts coverage and prices; any direct evidence that Massachusetts developers or attorneys will pay for alerts; how many solar and storage developers operate in the state; which website platforms towns use and those platforms' terms; whether later board minutes show CSL pass rates recovering or the exam extended to four hours; what code-book licenses cost; keyword search volumes; Stripe card fees; abatement filing volumes and the appraisal-licensing question; and whether the Attorney General publishes its own free WISP template. These gaps are why the plan starts with a two-week test rather than a full build.

## Conclusion

The research turns a common assumption about AI-built businesses around. If Claude can produce the product on request, so can every free AI-written competitor, which is exactly what has happened to WISP templates, appeal letters and practice questions. The defensible products are the ones where the AI's main job is upkeep: re-crawling hundreds of inconsistent town websites daily and fixing scrapers when they break, the tedious maintenance that keeps national incumbents thin in fragmented states like Massachusetts. Exam prep survives as a second product only because official records document an acute local pain, and it should be built on verified accuracy rather than question volume.

The other lesson concerns price more than product. At these base rates, a $49 consumer product needs more traffic than a new site can earn, while a $79–$299 business subscription needs a few dozen customers who can be found by name. Regulation supplies the timing: the October 1 permitting mandate and the spring 2027 town-meeting season open a window a national competitor could close, so the cheapest next step, two weeks testing Hamlet's coverage and collecting waitlist emails, is also the most valuable one.
