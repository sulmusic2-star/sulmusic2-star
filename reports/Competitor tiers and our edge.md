# Win every tier with dated proof

At every tier, we beat our rivals on one combination that none of the products reviewed here offers: siting items flagged **before the meeting**, at **town and township grain**, across **every board**, each carrying the agenda line, the source PDF and the time we first saw it, and delivered wherever the user already works. In the free tier, the closest rivals give away one board (LocalGovIndex) or one location (Citizen Portal), or they publish recaps after the vote (Red Tape, Hamlet, CivicSearch), and they keep alerts behind accounts or paywalls. Our planned free tier has no free equal: a home town plus its neighbours, every board, alerts by email, calendar, RSS or chat, free search, and no account. In the professional tier, every rival falls short in at least one way: it delivers by email only, works after the meeting, keeps webhooks and APIs for enterprise contracts or sales calls, or tracks no "proposed to adopted" status. So for developers, land-use lawyers and site selectors the win is a planned bundle of per-item proof, a published per-board coverage list and a status lifecycle, routed to Slack, Teams or a webhook for each watchlist. In the enterprise and data-licensing tier, siting platforms and energy-data majors learn about local rules by phoning counties, by reading minutes or video after the meeting, or by buying parcel and asset data. Nobody yet sells a pre-meeting event feed at town and township grain with published coverage, measured lead time and dated history, and that is the record we can supply. We lose on breadth (312 towns in three states, against rivals claiming 3,000 to 23,000 places), transcripts, human verification, mobile apps and security certification. Reading agendas is not an edge in itself, because at least eight paid products claim to do it. The edge that lasts is a dated archive of what each town posted and when, a public coverage registry, and permission to read sites that are closing to unverified bots. None of these can be bought after the fact. The main threats are funded aggregators reaching town grain in our states, cheap do-it-yourself AI agents, and agenda vendors closing access. In the next 90 days, to about January 10, 2027, we should start the history clock and publish the proof, lock in access before Indiana's December 31 notice deadline, and ship the free tier, the professional proof bundle and a data-licence front door.

## Free rivals send one board's agenda, or the story after the vote

Every product with a free tier fits one of three patterns. **Recap products** (Citizen Portal, Red Tape Recordings, CivicSearch, Hamlet, Documenters, Patch) tell residents what happened after the meeting. **One-place products** limit the free unit to a single board or location. LocalGovIndex's free plan covers "one board or council", Citizen Portal lets a free user "follow 1 location", and MyTownView lets anyone browse but charges for its first alerts ([LocalGovIndex pricing](https://localgovindex.com/pricing); [Citizen Portal pricing](https://citizenportal.ai/checkoutlifetime); [MyTownView pricing](https://mytownview.com/pricing)). **Keyword tools** such as Google Alerts, Visualping and newspaper notice searches do not know what an agenda is. Among products that read agendas, only two free options send anything before the meeting. LocalGovIndex emails an agenda summary for its one free board. Its $9 Civic plan widens the places but lists only recaps and "email change alerts", with no preview before the meeting ([LocalGovIndex pricing](https://localgovindex.com/pricing)). Town-run CivicPlus Notify Me and Granicus govDelivery lists email every agenda for one board, only after the resident signs in, with no topic filter ([Henry County, IN Notify Me](https://henrycounty.in.gov/List.aspx)). **No free product combines siting topics, every board, the neighbouring towns, alerts days before the meeting, and calendar, RSS or chat delivery without an account** ([free-tier inventory](<../research_notes/Product strategy/free_tier.md>)).

That gap matters because, in 2026 news coverage, residents complain loudest that they found out too late. In Spartanburg County, South Carolina, a resident learned of a data-center discussion about 30 minutes before the meeting, because "there was no mention of data centers, moratorium or anything listed in the agenda". A council member replied that agendas "only need to provide enough information about the topic being discussed" ([Fox Carolina](https://www.foxcarolina.com/2026/06/17/we-were-shocked-spartanburg-county-residents-criticize-handling-data-center-discussions/)). Neighbours of a data-center proposal in Kenilworth, New Jersey said they "got no notification" ([News12](https://newjersey.news12.com/anger-boils-over-as-kenilworth-moves-ahead-with-ai-data-center-proposal)). In League City, Texas, "a yard sale-like sign is how most residents found out" about a battery-storage plant ([ABC13](https://abc13.com/post/league-city-residents-proposed-battery-storage-facility-council-vote/14515634/)). A Berkeley Lab survey found that **only 17%** of people living within three miles of large solar projects knew of the project before construction ([LBNL/UM/MSU](https://escholarship.org/content/qt18w742c6/qt18w742c6.pdf)).

Late discovery has two causes. Sometimes nobody saw an agenda that was posted, and reading every board daily fixes that. Sometimes the agenda never named the topic, and no reader can fully fix that. Even posted agendas leave little time. In Massachusetts, one agenda in ten goes up only **2 days** before the meeting or later, and **4%** first appear on the day of the meeting ([where_we_win.md](<../research_notes/Product strategy/where_we_win.md>)).

### How the free tier wins for residents, reporters and town committees

The free tier answers each of the five recurring complaints with something already built or planned. **Against "too late"**, every board in the home town and its neighbours is read daily. Each item is flagged the day its agenda posts, stamped "posted N days before the meeting" and put on a calendar. The neighbour rule already has a basis in law. Massachusetts notifies "the planning board of every abutting city or town" ([c.40A §11](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleVII/Chapter40A/Section11)). New York notifies the clerk of the adjacent municipality at least ten days ahead for site plans, special permits and use variances within 500 feet ([GML §239-nn](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GMU/239-NN)). Virginia notifies the adjoining locality for land within half a mile ([§15.2-2204](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title15.2/chapter22/section15.2-2204/)). All three notify the neighbouring government, not its residents. Neighbour alerts reach the residents.

**Against noise**, alerts carry siting items only. Each neighbour item is labelled "next door in [Town]", and the resident can remove it per town or per topic. Nextdoor shows why this matters: reviewers complain the app "keeps spamming me with notifications for neighboring towns" ([GetApp](https://www.getapp.com/collaboration-software/a/nextdoor/)).

**Against paywalls on public information**, alerts stay free and are never delayed on purpose. Elsewhere, Colorado charges for notice emails, Citizen Portal puts topic alerts on Pro, Hamlet sends notifications only in a trial, and MyTownView starts alerts on a paid plan.

**Against sign-up friction**, one link covers a whole watch. Email works by double opt-in, unsubscribing works per watch, and calendar, RSS and chat need no sign-up at all. Town lists instead require a sign-in for each board in each town.

**Against AI errors**, every alert quotes the agenda line word for word, names the board, date and page, and links the PDF. Citizen Portal, by contrast, corrected an AI article that wrote "lane" for "lien", and it carries an "AI makes mistakes" disclaimer ([Citizen Portal](https://cp.soar.com/articles/6720514/Lindsay-City/Tulare-County/California/Council-approves-working-items-in-single-consent-motion)).

The channels are a quieter win. Visualping keeps Slack and Teams off its free plan, Talkwalker is the only rival that gives Slack away, and no free rival offers a calendar subscription. Google Calendar refreshes subscribed feeds on its own schedule, reportedly up to 12–24 hours, which suits hearings that are days away ([AddCal](https://help.addcal.co/en/articles/11933752-calendar-subscriber-update-delay)). Sharing is the distribution channel. **52%** of US adults get local news from community forums such as Facebook groups or Nextdoor, against 36% from a daily paper ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2026/04/14/local-news-fact-sheet/)). That makes item pages that preview well in those groups worth more than any sign-up form.

### Where the free tier loses to national rivals and town platforms

Coverage is the biggest weakness, more than any missing feature. We read 312 towns in Massachusetts, New York and Rhode Island ([our site](https://351watch.vercel.app/)). Red Tape claims 7,334 governments, GatherGov 9,300+ and MyTownView 23,000+, so most US residents who visit us today get nothing. A visible coverage map, a "request my town" button and the honest count the site already shows ("240 of 351") matter more than new features. We also lose on transcripts and video, which we should not try to match: we should link to Red Tape or Citizen Portal for what was said. We have no mobile app or push alerts. Our daily read is slower than Visualping's hourly checks. We cover siting topics only, so no budgets or schools. And we cannot flag what an agenda never names. The partial answer is to flag generic land items such as "zoning text amendment", "development agreement" or "executive session re: land acquisition", and to read packets and minutes where they are posted.

No public reviews exist for LocalGovIndex, MyTownView, Hamlet, GatherGov, Red Tape or CivicSearch. Their accuracy is untested, not proven good ([free-tier competitor notes](<../research_notes/Competitor tiers and our edge/free_tier_competitors.md>)).

The incumbent move most likely to dent the free tier is a town platform adding AI keyword filters to its own Notify Me or govDelivery lists. Granicus already lists resident Q&A over meetings ("GXA for Public Meetings") and full-text portal search as available ([Granicus innovation](https://granicus.com/innovation/)). Each of these is limited to one customer government. A filtered single-town alert from the town would undercut us for single-town residents. It would not touch the neighbouring towns, multi-board coverage, the delivery channels or the no-account design.

### What developers, lawyers and site selectors get that no rival packages

Published reviews of local-agenda monitors are thin. Neighbouring categories (project leads, ownership data, bill tracking) show what professionals reward and punish, and the order is consistent. **Accuracy and freshness come first.** ConstructConnect reviewers call its information "not updated and sometimes misleading" ([G2](https://www.g2.com/products/constructconnect-project-intelligence/reviews)), and Reonomy draws the "no researchers to confirm accuracy" complaint ([CRE Daily](https://www.credaily.com/reviews/reonomy-review/)). **Completeness where decisions happen comes second.** Build's site-selection guide calls zoning "the most fragmented data layer" and says "there is no substitute for a direct call to the planning department" ([Build.inc](https://build.inc/insights/site-selection-data-sources-guide)). Carina warns that moratoriums "can pass within a single board meeting cycle and may not appear in databases for weeks or months" ([Carina](https://carina.energy/bess-permitting-guide/)).

Entitlement risk now ranks alongside power for data-center buyers. A NAIOP panel of Newmark, Link Logistics and others said "entitlement issues are far less predictable" and that unresolved entitlement questions create "more concern than difficult infrastructure challenges" ([CREDA](https://blog.credaglobal.org/2026/05/rethinking-power-entitlement-risk-and-execution-in-an-increasingly-complex-data-center-market/)). Next come alerts that are easy to set up: FiscalNote reviewers want a way to "star" or "flag" high-importance items ([Software Advice](https://www.softwareadvice.com/collaboration/fiscalnote-profile)). Flexible terms, unlimited users, exports and responsive support follow. AI summaries and maps appear nowhere in the praise or the complaints, because every rival has them ([professional-tier notes](<../research_notes/Competitor tiers and our edge/pro_tier_competitors.md>)).

That evidence points to one bundle that no rival shows in full. It has three parts: **proof on every item** (the source PDF, the time we first saw it, and a public correction log), **a published per-board coverage list** that includes towns and townships, and **a status lifecycle** running from proposed through hearing, continuance, vote and adoption to appeal or state review. Around that core sit routing to Slack, Teams or a webhook per watchlist, company watch from LLC to parent, and exports with stable IDs. Here is what that means for each buyer.

**A permitting manager at a community solar or battery developer** watching 50 towns today faces about 150 town subscriptions, one per board per town ([where_we_win.md](<../research_notes/Product strategy/where_we_win.md>)), or LocalGovIndex Professional's email. We would give one feed: battery items to the storage team's channel, data-center items to another, and every item linked to its agenda page. Company watch would catch a competitor's single-project LLC. In Massachusetts wetlands filings, New Leaf Energy stands behind at least 9 of 36 commercial solar and battery filings, and 34 solar or battery agenda items in 13 towns name only an LLC ([where_we_win.md](<../research_notes/Product strategy/where_we_win.md>)).

**A land-use lawyer** gets an item that can be cited (board, date, page, PDF and first-seen time) and a decision history running from the agenda to Attorney General review or appeal. The lawyer can also forward alerts and exports to named clients, which LocalGovIndex's "commercial internal-use" licence forbids below Enterprise ([pricing](https://localgovindex.com/pricing)). OrdinanceWatch's Premium+ tier shows that law firms pay for client-facing alerts, but it covers only Florida and Georgia.

**A site selector** gets lead time: in Massachusetts we first saw flagged items a median of **3.2 days** before the meeting. Our first crawl ran on October 10, so this measures our first fetch, not the town's posting date. GatherGov's records arrive within 24 hours after the meeting. The site selector also gets a coverage list that shows whether silence means "nothing posted" or "not read".

**Consultants and engineers** join a developer's workspace as free external collaborators. BillTrack50's "unlimited users, including external people" shows the model works ([pricing](https://www.billtrack50.com/info/pricing)).

All of these professional features are planned. Today the product delivers by RSS, JSON, calendar and Slack, Teams or Discord, with first-seen times recorded per item ([our site](https://351watch.vercel.app/)).

The easiest rivals to displace in a one-week trial are GatherGov (works after the meeting), Hamlet (it lacks towns and source links), Citizen Portal (consumer) and LocalGovIndex Professional (email only, internal-use licence). Against those three we should be the siting layer they lack and connect to them through exports, webhooks and an API rather than try to replace them. Four rival features are worth copying outright: OrdinanceWatch's white-label client alerts for law firms, BillTrack50's flat model with unlimited external users, LocalGovIndex's stable IDs and change feed, and the AI-agent (MCP) connectors that Civic IQ and dcmap already offer.

None of these comparisons was tested with an account, so the rivals' lead times and accuracy are their own claims. If GatherGov turns out to offer Slack and webhooks under its demo-sold plan, our routing advantage over it shrinks.

### What licensees gain from us

For platforms and majors, we would be better in five specific ways. **First, our records carry a time from before the meeting.** A record built from phone calls or minutes cannot show one, so our first-seen times are proof that no incumbent can backfill.

**Second, we have a cheap, lawful route to township grain where townships decide.** In Ohio, a township must send a proposed zoning amendment to the county or regional planning commission "within five days after the adoption of the motion". It then sets its hearing "not less than twenty nor more than forty days" out ([ORC 519.12](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-519.12)). Michigan gives the county 30 days to review a township's proposed ordinance ([MCL 125.3307](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-125-3307)). By my estimate, reading roughly 88 Ohio and 83 Michigan county planning bodies reveals township actions across thousands of townships weeks ahead.

**Third, we cover many topics.** Carina covers battery storage, and Savrn and Data Center Watch cover data centers. We tag solar, battery storage, data centers, moratoria, zoning, towers, wetlands and housing in one schema.

**Fourth, we would publish the licence package buyers check before they sign.** That means history fixed at the time it was recorded, coverage proof, methodology, delivery in their own systems, accuracy evidence and stable IDs. As one alternative-data guide puts it, "methodology and point-in-time integrity almost never come up in the first call and almost always come up before signature" ([Guideline](https://www.guideline.ai/capital-markets-seo/alternative-data-vendor-due-diligence)).

**Fifth, we replace labour.** On Heatmap's salary band, my arithmetic puts the cost of covering 3,100 counties by phone at quarterly depth at six to ten researchers, roughly $0.5–0.8 million a year, before any township. That is the in-house cost a licence displaces.

A full licence package would include:

| Component | Contents |
|---|---|
| Stable IDs | Keyed to Census codes for each town, county and board |
| Topic and action fields | What the item is about and what kind of action it is |
| Applicant | Linked to its parent company |
| Lifecycle | Each status change with a timestamp and source; records are only added, never overwritten |
| Lead-time fields | First seen, posted, meeting and decision times |
| Coverage list | Every body read, with its gap log |
| Accuracy audit | Quarterly, sampled and published |
| History | Older records labelled "backfilled" and dated to when they were captured |
| Delivery | API, webhooks, Snowflake or S3 drops, CSV and MCP |

Lead as a **supplier** to these platforms, and keep a direct product for the buyers the platforms do not price for: community solar and battery developers, counsel and consultants. The platforms already pay for this data with labour, and the majors showed in 2026 that they buy rather than build. Being a supplier to Wood Mackenzie, S&P or Enverus is also the usual route to acquisition.

Three risks are real. **Copying**: Spark and LocalGovIndex already crawl. **Channel conflict**: a direct tier sells to the platforms' own customers. **Concentration**: one large licensee can come to dominate revenue. Each has a defence:

| Risk | Defence |
|---|---|
| Copying | License the records, not the coverage map or source list; keep crawl schedules private; hold dated history a late copier cannot recreate |
| Channel conflict | Separate the products by shape: bulk records and APIs for platforms, town-level alerts for direct buyers |
| Concentration | Non-exclusive licences by default; exclusivity only per state and for a limited time |

No platform publicly says it licenses local-decision data from a third party. Their willingness to buy is inferred from the labour they spend and the companies they acquire. It has not been observed.

## Dated history and published proof are the only edges money cannot buy later

Several things are not edges. Reading agendas before the meeting is claimed by MultiState, Quorum, FiscalNote, Hamlet, Kite, Spark, Civic IQ and OrdinanceWatch ([competitor coverage map](<../research_notes/Meeting monitor national expansion/competitor_coverage_map.md>)). Three things should stay off the plan. Do not build transcripts or AI meeting recaps: rivals give them away and buyers do not reward them. Do not claim to be "the only" monitor that works before the meeting, because at least eight products say they read agendas. Do not create an account on any town or vendor site to read agendas, because signing up can bind us to terms that would undo the legal posture above ([moats and threats notes](<../research_notes/Competitor tiers and our edge/moats_and_threats.md>)).

And no one knows yet whether residents prefer the quoted agenda line to an AI summary. The trial weeks and the free-tier usage data answer all three.

## Conclusion

The three tiers sell the same record at three levels of proof. The free tier sells the alert, the professional tier adds the evidence and the status, and the licence sells the evidence at scale with its history. The free tier therefore does more than drive sign-ups. It runs the collection and citation engine that makes the paid tiers credible. Every free watch shows which towns people care about, every shared item page earns a link, and every reported miss improves the record that licensees will audit. That is why the free tier can be generous at almost no marginal cost. Withholding facts from residents would weaken the proof that professionals and platforms pay for.

The second shift is that small, published coverage beats large, unpublished coverage for the buyers who matter. A developer or platform deciding whether silence means "nothing was posted" or "nobody read it" can trust a checkable list of 312 towns with dated lead times more than a claim of 12,214 places with no list behind it. Rivals can add towns quickly. They cannot add last year's timestamps. Every week without fingerprinted copies of each agenda is history that is lost for good, and LocalGovIndex is already linking agendas to outcomes nationally. So the deadline that matters most is not a competitor's launch date. It is the day we start recording.
