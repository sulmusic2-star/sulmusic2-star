# Expand Where Township Siting Votes Go Unwatched

351 Watch should expand in three waves across five regions rather than into one cluster. **Wave 1** takes the **southern Michigan township belt**, **Virginia's Southside and Piedmont counties**, **Utah statewide** through its public notice portal, and **Maine towns plus the Vermont and Connecticut state siting dockets**. **Wave 2** adds **Ohio's solar-belt and data-center townships**, **Pennsylvania's township belts**, **Indiana counties** and **Georgia's exurban and rural counties**. **Wave 3** covers Illinois, Wisconsin–Minnesota, the Carolinas and Kentucky, the Plains, and the Texas, Colorado and New Mexico data-center counties, and starts only after vendor allow-listing or a press-association notice feed cuts the cost of reading them. The people with the most to gain and nothing comparable locally fall in two places. One is developers, counsel and residents in the Great Lakes and Pennsylvania townships. There, Hamlet lists only **2–4% of local governments** and has no township type in its data model, and the only visible township coverage is written after the meeting. The other is residents of single-source counties in Georgia, Texas, Virginia, Ohio and rural Utah, where notice is thinnest and some multi-billion-dollar data centers, such as Juab County's, were approved before neighbors heard of them. The buyers who need the service most are **data-center developers** (a 60 MW site loses about **$14.2M per month of delay**) and **community-scale solar and battery developers** (a cancelled solar project sinks **more than $2M**), followed by utility-scale developers reached through platform licences and developer-side law firms. Cost sets the order within that demand. Today's adapters read only **31% of 1,737 reachable county websites**, Utah hosts every county's and city's agenda files on one state portal, and only an estimated **15–50% of townships in Ohio, Pennsylvania and Michigan post agendas online at all**. Data centers should lead the subject list everywhere, including their tax deals, annexations, on-site gas plants and transmission lines; battery storage and solar moratoria come next, and housing permits and cell towers stay as tags outside 40B-style states. Prices can hold at **$349/mo for a Siting Desk sold per region, $499/mo for the data-center feed and $6–12k per state per year for platform licences**. But LocalGovIndex already publishes a **$399/mo** plan with pre-meeting agenda summaries, so the selling point must be published, place-by-place completeness and measured lead time, not agenda reading itself. No price has been tested with a buyer, and the cost tiers and composite scores below are judgments built on verified counts.

## Two different gaps: unwatched townships and news-starved counties

No product shows pre-meeting coverage of siting items at town or township grain in the states where townships hold zoning power. Hamlet is the only rival that publishes a per-state breakdown. It lists **55 places in Pennsylvania (2.1% of 2,625 land-use governments), 80 in Ohio (3.4%), 76 in Michigan (4.1%), 55 in Wisconsin (2.9%) and 75 in Minnesota (2.8%)**, against 30–60% of governments in California, Florida, Arizona and Nevada ([Hamlet coverage](https://www.myhamlet.com/coverage); shares estimated in [competitor_coverage_map.md](<../research_notes/Meeting monitor national expansion/competitor_coverage_map.md>)). Its API recognizes only city, county and state locations, with no town or township type ([Hamlet API](https://api.myhamlet.com/openapi.json)).

The rivals that reach further stop short in other ways. **Citizen Portal** is the only product with visible rural-township siting stories, such as Williams County, Ohio trustees' solar resolutions and a Warriors Mark Township, Pennsylvania solar hearing, but both are recaps written after the meeting ([Citizen Portal, Williams Co.](https://cpo.soar.com/articles/6023790/Ohio/Township-trustees-press-commissioners-for-tools-to-limit-large-solar-fields); [Warriors Mark](https://citizenportal.ai/articles/9335109/pennsylvania/huntingdon-county/warriors-mark/township-schedules-solar-conditionaluse-hearing-motions-to-prompt-ordinance-update)). **GatherGov** makes items searchable "within 24 hours of the meeting" ([GatherGov](https://gathergov.com/articles/gathergov-curate-alternative-in-2026)), **Quorum** stops at places of 3,000 people or more ([Quorum Local](https://www.quorum.us/products/local/)), **Spark** limits township coverage to places "where public online sources exist" ([Spark](https://www.sparkhq.ai/)), and **Heatmap Pro** builds its local-law data by phoning counties ([Heatmap Pro](https://heatmap.news/pro)). Pennsylvania's only state-specific resource, **Penn State's database**, records adopted ordinances for 300 governments, after the fact ([Penn State CELP](https://celp.psu.edu/solarordinance/about)). The competitor research therefore concludes that no product with rural-township reach reads agendas before the meeting, and no pre-meeting product shows rural-township reach ([competitor_coverage_map.md](<../research_notes/Meeting monitor national expansion/competitor_coverage_map.md>)).

The data-center trackers confirm the hole by disagreeing with each other. In Michigan, Electric Choice counts **63** restrictions, dcmap **0** and GatherGov "20 or more" ([Electric Choice](https://www.electricchoice.com/datacenters/moratoriums/); [dcmap](https://dcmap.us/insights/policy/); [GatherGov tracker](https://gathergov.com/articles/data-center-moratorium-2026-tracker)). Nationally, counts of local actions run from 47 at dcmap to **1,233** at Savrn, which calls its count "a floor" ([Savrn](https://savrn.com/data-center-moratorium-tracker)).

The newest threat is **LocalGovIndex**. It reads agendas and minutes for **2,823 local governments** and sends pre-meeting agenda summaries ([LocalGovIndex data centers](https://localgovindex.com/issues/data-centers)). That is about 7% of the roughly 38,700 counties, municipalities and townships in the US ([Census 2025, Table 1](https://cdn.www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/publications/2025/econ/govtorg2225.pdf); my arithmetic). Its coverage page was not read, so its depth in the Michigan, Ohio and Pennsylvania townships is unknown.

The information gap sits mostly elsewhere, in the county-grain states of the South, the Plains and the interior West. Medill counts **212 counties with no local news source and 1,525 with exactly one**. They are home to almost 50 million people, and almost 80% of the news deserts are rural ([Medill 2025](https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/assets/slnp/the_state_of_local_news_2025.pdf)). The most people in zero- or one-source counties live in **Georgia (5 million), Texas (4 million), North Carolina (2.9 million) and Kentucky (2.38 million)**. Maryland, New Jersey, Maine, Hawaii and Ohio have lost the largest share of their newspapers since 2005 (same source). In Virginia, the number of news-desert counties more than doubled between 2023 and 2025, to 16 ([Axios Richmond](https://www.axios.com/local/richmond/2026/04/06/virginia-news-deserts-rise-local-newspaper-closures)).

Notice law is moving the same way. After Florida let local governments post legal notices on county websites, a working paper found newspaper hearing notices fell **44%** and zoning notices **27%**. The county websites saw no traffic gain, and the number of people speaking at meetings fell **14–18%** ([Yale SOM](https://insights.som.yale.edu/insights/removing-government-notices-from-newspapers-reduces-citizen-participation-in-decision)). Other states have followed. An Ohio township may now notice a rezoning hearing only on "the web site and social media account of the township" ([ORC 519.12](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-519.12)), New Jersey dropped its newspaper mandate in March 2026 ([NJ Monitor](https://newjerseymonitor.com/2025/07/01/new-jersey-removes-mandate-to-publish-legal-notices-in-newspapers/)), and Indiana makes newspaper notice optional from July 1, 2027 ([PNRC](https://www.pnrc.net/2026/03/03/march-2026-monthly-newsletter/)). Even the strongest hearing-notice rules reach few people. Michigan mails notice to owners and occupants within 300 feet, 15 days ahead ([MCL 125.3103](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-125-3103)). Virginia mails it 5 days ahead to the owners and abutters ([Va. Code 15.2-2204](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title15.2/chapter22/section15.2-2204/)).

Where no hearing is required at all, the gap is total. Texas counties have no general zoning power, and Angelina County asked the Legislature for authority even "to mandate a public hearing process" for large data-center and battery projects ([KLTV](https://www.kltv.com/2026/07/14/angelina-county-commissioners-vote-ask-state-authority-regulate-data-centers/)). In Juab County, Utah, the permit for the $10B+ Nebo North data center was approved at a four-minute hearing that no resident attended. A neighbor learned of it four months later ([Deseret News](https://www.deseret.com/environment/2026/09/08/mona-citizens-revolt-against-local-data-centers/)).

Demand concentrates in a third set of places, which overlaps both gaps unevenly. The hotspot analysis tabulated the LBNL end-2025 queue, the live PJM queue and the Sabin Center's 866 local restrictions, then ranked 22 sub-state clusters. Its top tier is southern Michigan townships, Southside Virginia, the central Ohio solar belt, the Michigan Thumb, east-central Indiana and central Pennsylvania ([energy_siting_hotspots.md](<../research_notes/Meeting monitor national expansion/energy_siting_hotspots.md>); data from the [LBNL queue file](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2026-05/lbnl_ix_queue_data_file_thru2025.xlsx), [PJM queue XML](https://www.pjm.com/pub/planning/downloads/xml/PlanningQueues.xml) and [Sabin database](https://oppositionreport.org/reports/current/)).

Data-center fights peak in the same Great Lakes and Pennsylvania townships, and also in Georgia's metro ring and the North Carolina Piedmont. Heatmap counted **nearly 190 severe local data-center restrictions in roughly 60 days** of mid-2026. It puts the cancellation rate for contested projects at **about 71% in Michigan**, 56% in Indiana and 17% in Texas ([Heatmap](https://heatmap.news/politics/data-center-local-laws-bans-total)).

The two gaps are not the same gap. In the township belts, the paying buyers have no pre-meeting product at town grain. In the single-source counties of the South and West, developers already have county-grain tools (Heatmap, Spark, SitePath), but residents have almost nothing. A portfolio that serves only one gap leaves either revenue or the public record behind. The second gap also matters commercially, because the citations, permalinks and public data it generates are what the paying segments read and trust. That makes it the distribution layer for the first ([who_needs_it_most.md](<../research_notes/Meeting monitor national expansion/who_needs_it_most.md>)).

## Developers lose $14 million a month to delay, and only 17% of solar neighbors knew in time

Two segments tie at the top of the national needs ranking. Both watch many jurisdictions at once and lose heavily from a single missed vote ([who_needs_it_most.md](<../research_notes/Meeting monitor national expansion/who_needs_it_most.md>)).

The first is **data-center developers and site selectors**. There are **389** developers with US projects ([Cleanview](https://cleanview.co/data-centers/us)), a typical 60 MW site loses about **$14.2M per month** of delay ([STL Partners](https://stlpartners.com/press/delays-in-data-centre-construction/)), and Heatmap counts at least **$260B** of 2026 data-center investment canceled after local opposition through September ([Heatmap](https://heatmap.news/daily/260-billion-data-center-opposition)). The second is **community-scale solar and battery (BESS) developers**. **150** firms have operating storage projects ([Cleanview BESS](https://cleanview.co/battery-storage-projects/us)), and battery moratoria and restrictions now cover **174 jurisdictions in 16 states** ([Carina](https://carina.energy/bess-moratoriums/)), against 37 moratoria in 12 states in mid-2024 ([PNNL](https://www.pnnl.gov/sites/default/files/media/file/Supporting%20Energy%20Storage%20in%20Siting%20Proceedings.pdf)); the two counts use different methods. Developers report that about a third of wind and solar siting applications were cancelled, with local ordinances and zoning the top cause ([Latitude Media on LBNL](https://latitudemedia.com/news/heres-whats-holding-back-solar-and-wind-developers)), and each cancelled solar project sinks more than $2M ([LBNL](https://emp.lbl.gov/news/larg-e-scale-wind-and-solar)).

The harm runs both ways, which is why one early, neutral feed has buyers on both sides. Residents mostly learn too late. A Berkeley Lab survey of people within three miles of large solar projects found **only 17% knew of the project before construction**, and more than a third did not know it existed even afterward ([LBNL/UM/MSU](https://escholarship.org/content/qt18w742c6/qt18w742c6.pdf)). In Sand Springs, Oklahoma, a resident learned of an 827-acre annexation for a future Google data center the day after the council approved it ([The Frontier](https://www.readfrontier.org/stories/how-data-center-developers-won-secrecy-pledges-from-oklahoma-officials/)).

Developers are blindsided by the same gaps. A court voided Prince William County's Digital Gateway rezonings, about 2,100 acres, because the county's own advertised notice was defective ([WTOP](https://wtop.com/prince-william-county/2025/08/judge-voids-digital-gateway-rezoning-in-prince-william-county/)). Hill County, Texas passed a moratorium 3–2. A developer holding more than $80M of land contracts sued for over $100M, and the county rescinded the moratorium within a month ([Texas Tribune](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/06/05/texas-hill-county-moratorium-rescinded-data-centers/)).

The cost of a missed hearing also varies by state, and that should steer where the paid product goes first. It is highest where one vote ends a whole class of projects or moves where they are decided. In Ohio a county resolution under SB 52 can ban large wind and solar, as Crawford County's did when Honey Creek Wind was canceled ([Sabin database](https://oppositionreport.org/reports/current/)). In Michigan a township moratorium leaves the township without a "compatible renewable energy ordinance" under PA 233, which lets the developer go to the state commission instead ([Court of Appeals opinion](https://www.michbar.org/Portals/0/opinions/appeals/2026/050726/85698.pdf); [Fort Gratiot outline](https://fortgratiot.us/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Outline-Public-Act-233-of-2023.pdf)). Indiana's Dearborn County moratorium took effect the day after the vote ([WVXU](https://www.wvxu.org/environment/2026-02-25/dearborn-county-moratorium-solar-farms-data-center)), and Virginia's Mecklenburg County capped utility-scale solar acreage, then dropped it as a future use, leaving pending projects racing for the remaining cap ([Cardinal News](https://cardinalnews.org/2025/04/15/mecklenburg-county-blocks-future-large-scale-solar-development/)). The cost is lowest where state law limits counties. Illinois counties must now issue permits to compliant solar projects ([ISACo](https://www.isacoil.org/News/30112/Illinois-Appellate-Court-Affirms-Limitations-of-County-Authority-Over-Solar-Projects/news-detail/)), and Texas counties have no zoning power.

None of these segments needs email to be reached. The cheapest path to developers is a data licence to the platforms they already pay, since Heatmap and Carina both spend labor on what 351 Watch automates. The free segments are reached through shareable town pages: Pew finds **52%** of adults get local news from community forums such as Facebook groups or Nextdoor, against **36%** from a daily paper ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2026/04/14/local-news-fact-sheet/)). Clean Grid Alliance's 70+ members span nine Upper Midwest states and include developers and farmer organizations ([CGA](https://cleangridalliance.org/membership/members)). A member-resource listing there rewards breadth across the Great Lakes rather than depth in one state.

## One portal covers Utah, while most Ohio townships post no agenda online

The cost of coverage varies between states far more than demand does. A census probed all **1,982 county governments in 26 states** with the crawler's own polite fetcher, which identifies itself as 351WatchBot, obeys robots.txt and never solves challenges ([coverage_feasibility.md](<../research_notes/Meeting monitor national expansion/coverage_feasibility.md>); [coverage_county_census.csv](<../research_notes/Meeting monitor national expansion/coverage_county_census.csv>)). Of **1,737 reachable** county sites, **268 (15%)** are readable by the four stock adapters and **270 (16%)** more by hand-curated listings, while **293 (17%)** sit behind bot walls and **72 (4%)** use agenda vendors that refuse polite bots (Granicus, BoardDocs, PrimeGov, Diligent/iCompass, iQM2 and Destiny). Another **628 (36%)** showed no agenda within two link levels, a lower bound, since a deeper re-probe found agendas at 11 of 84 such counties. The vendor refusals are explicit. Granicus ends its robots file with `User-agent: * Disallow: /` ([DeKalb Granicus robots](https://dekalbcountyga.granicus.com/robots.txt)), and BoardDocs returns a 403 even for robots.txt ([BoardDocs](https://go.boarddocs.com/robots.txt)). **No candidate state reaches 60% of its siting bodies on today's adapters alone.**

The exceptions are states with one statewide filing point, the pattern that let a single adapter cover all 39 Rhode Island municipalities. There, agendas are filed a median of 5.2 days before the meeting ([ri_portal.md](<../research_notes/Expansion/ri_portal.md>)). Utah's Public Notice Website lists all 29 counties and 270 municipal entities, hosts their agenda files at plain addresses and allows crawling in its robots file ([Utah PMN](https://www.utah.gov/pmn/); [Millard County notices](https://www.utah.gov/pmn/list/notices.html?id=1846&page=0)). Nevada law requires every public body to post its agenda notice on the state site by 9 a.m. on the third working day before a meeting ([NRS 241](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-241.html); [notice.nv.gov](https://notice.nv.gov/)). Vermont's daily Act 250 agenda lists every noticed project statewide ([Act 250 agenda](https://anrweb.vt.gov/ANR/Act250/agenda.aspx)), Connecticut's Siting Council keeps a reachable pending-matters page ([CT Siting Council](https://portal.ct.gov/csc/1_applications-and-other-pending-matters/pending-matters)), and five of six probed Maine towns ran on the stock CivicPlus code ([northeast_feasibility.md](<../research_notes/Meeting monitor deep dive/northeast_feasibility.md>)).

In the Great Lakes and Pennsylvania, townships set the cost, and how many post anything online falls steeply from state to state. In Michigan, **165 of 211 randomly drawn townships (78%)** list a website in the Michigan Townships Association directory, rising from 57% for townships under 1,000 people to 94% at 2,500–10,000 and 100% above 10,000 ([coverage_mi_township_websites.csv](<../research_notes/Meeting monitor national expansion/coverage_mi_township_websites.csv>); [MTA directory example](https://michigantownships.org/find-a-township/?fips=69240)). Only 4 of 8 random Pennsylvania townships had a website, and only 2 of 8 in Ohio. A website is not an agenda: only 4 of 17 random township sites showed a current agenda listing. On that evidence, the feasibility research estimates that **35–50% of Michigan townships, 30–45% in Pennsylvania and 15–25% in Ohio** post agendas online, and neither Ohio nor Michigan law requires an agenda at all ([coverage_feasibility.md](<../research_notes/Meeting monitor national expansion/coverage_feasibility.md>), Q2; [ORC 121.22](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-121.22)).

Places in active siting fights sit well above these averages. Limerick, Falls, East Whiteland and Archbald in Pennsylvania, and Liberty, Etna, New Albany, Miami and Washington in Ohio, all had current, readable agendas (same source). That supports a "hotspot-first" scope: cover the named bodies in each cluster and publish the list, rather than claim a whole state.

The scale is manageable. Southern Michigan and the Thumb hold about **850 bodies** (30 counties, 257 municipalities and 562 townships, per the Census tabulation in the hotspot notes). At the NY pilot's 10–20 minutes per hand listing, curating the roughly three-quarters that have websites takes about **110–220 hours** (my estimate), while Virginia's two clusters need only 49 deciding counties, 24 of them already readable.

The state permit systems that could give a MassDEP-style early warning are mostly closed to bots. Robots.txt shuts out Pennsylvania's eFACTS ([eFACTS](https://www.ahs.dep.pa.gov/eFACTSWeb/)), Michigan's MiEnviro ([robots](https://mienviro.michigan.gov/robots.txt)) and Georgia's regional-impact review system, the most valuable closed source for data centers ([GA DRI](https://apps.dca.ga.gov/DRI/Submissions.aspx)). Three state and regional sources are open and current: Minnesota's weekly EQB Monitor, which carries data-center environmental reviews ([EQB Monitor](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/MNEQB/bulletins/42d3d41)); SPP's queue CSV with county names ([SPP](https://opsportal.spp.org/Studies/GenerateActiveCSV)); and New York's DEC permit API ([coverage_feasibility.md](<../research_notes/Meeting monitor national expansion/coverage_feasibility.md>), Q4).

Three levers would cut cost in every High-tier state at once. The first is **vendor allow-listing**, with one deal each for Granicus (ViewPublisher, iQM2, PrimeGov) and Diligent (BoardDocs, iCompass). The second is **a paid feed from the press associations' shared public-notice platform**, whose sites are reachable in about 20 states. The third is **a crawl-delay policy that honors long delays** at a few requests per host per day, which alone adds about 50 counties, mostly in Indiana, where [Hendricks County's robots file](https://www.co.hendricks.in.us/robots.txt) asks for 300 seconds.

Spreading across groups hedges three risks that a single cluster would carry. The first is **policy risk**: each wave-1 region rests on a different state's siting law, and every one of those laws is moving. Michigan's Supreme Court could settle PA 233 ([Foster Swift](https://www.fosterswift.com/newsroom/news/michigan-supreme-court-zoning-control-renewable-energy-pa233-mpsc-appeal)), Pennsylvania's HB 502 would preempt local land use for projects of 25 MW and up if it moved, Virginia's 2026 HB 711 and SB 443 shift some solar criteria and co-located storage away from local discretion ([Beveridge & Diamond](https://www.bdlaw.com/publications/environmental-highlights-from-virginias-2026-legislative-session/)), and Illinois courts now order counties to permit compliant solar. The second is **subject risk**: new renewable restrictions are slowing (165, then 112, then 70 a year) while battery and data-center restrictions climb ([pv magazine](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/09/08/the-u-s-saw-a-14-percent-increase-in-contested-renewable-projects-9-8-gw-in-cancellations-in-2025/)), so energy-led groups (Michigan, Virginia) should sit beside data-center-led ones (Utah, the SE Michigan townships, Pennsylvania's gas campuses). The third is **build risk**: Wave 1 deliberately exercises three adapter families (generic township listings, county CivicPlus/CivicClerk sites and a state portal), and whichever proves cheapest can carry Wave 2.

Each wave should pass a gate before the next begins. The gate levels are my estimates, modeled on the earlier where-we-win kill rule. Each region should publish its per-place coverage list and measured lead times, reach at least half of the listed Michigan hotspot bodies (and at least 60% elsewhere), and show trials from at least five firms active in the region, or one platform licence, within six months. Wave 2's High-tier states should also wait for one vendor allow-listing or notice-feed agreement.

Four findings would change the order. If LocalGovIndex turns out to cover the Michigan or Ohio townships in depth, the gap there narrows. If Utah's or Nevada's portal terms bar automated use, the cheap complete-state claim fails; those terms have not been read, unlike Rhode Island's. If the Michigan Supreme Court broadly upholds the state route, Michigan's local stakes fall. And if Georgia grants access to its regional-review system, Georgia moves into Wave 1.

## Conclusion

The township states' high cost and their competitor gap are one fact seen from two sides. Michigan, Ohio and Pennsylvania are unserved because thousands of small, inconsistently posted townships are expensive to read, and every rival has stopped at the county or the city. The defensible asset is therefore not the crawler but the published, place-by-place list of what was read and when. Rivals claim townships only in general terms, and LocalGovIndex's 2,823 governments cannot yet cover these belts in depth. Revenue and need are also geographically split. The firms that pay are concentrated where township votes kill projects. The people most harmed by late discovery are concentrated in the single-source counties of the South and West. Pairing Michigan with Virginia and Utah sells to the first group while building, for free, the public record and citations that the first group trusts.

The window is also dated. Pennsylvania's Act 44 applies from January 2027, Indiana's newspaper notice becomes optional in July 2027, and Michigan's Supreme Court appeal on PA 233 is pending. A service already reading these governments' own websites when notice moves off newspapers becomes, in practice, the notice record itself.
