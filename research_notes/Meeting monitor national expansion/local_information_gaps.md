## 1. Which counties are news deserts or have only one local source? How many, which states have the most, and where are papers disappearing fastest?

### Takeaway
Medill's 2025 report counts 212 news-desert counties and 1,525 more with only one local source, usually a weekly paper. Together they hold about 50 million people. Measured by population in these zero- or one-source counties, the worst-off states are Georgia, Texas, North Carolina and Kentucky. Mississippi has the highest share of residents with limited access. Virginia's news deserts doubled between 2023 and 2025. By percentage of papers lost, Maryland, New Jersey, Maine, Hawaii and Ohio have lost the most. I found no 2026 edition yet; Medill was still collecting nominations for it.

### Inferences
- Thin local news coverage is mainly a South, Plains and Mountain West problem: Georgia, Texas, Virginia, Tennessee, Kentucky, Mississippi, Utah and Idaho. The Northeast states 351 Watch covers now are comparatively well served, so national expansion should move toward the South and Interior West.
- The number of news deserts understates the problem. Single-source counties are more than seven times as numerous, and that one source is usually a weekly paper with little capacity to read agendas.
- The Aurelia Star closure shows a feedback loop. Taking public notices away from a paper can close it, which deepens the information gap in that county.

## 2. Which states changed public-notice rules in 2022–2026, and what do land-use hearing notices require?

### Takeaway
Since 2022, Florida (2022), Ohio (2023), New Jersey (2025, in force March 2026) and Indiana (2026, effective 2027) have enacted laws letting governments publish legal notices on their own websites or other non-newspaper channels. The main empirical study of Florida's change found 44% fewer newspaper hearing notices, 27% fewer zoning notices and 14–18% fewer public speakers at meetings. Statutory land-use notice is short and narrow: usually 5–15 days of mailed notice, sent only to abutters or owners within 200–300 feet, plus one or two newspaper ads. Texas counties have no general zoning power, so many rural data-center and battery projects there get no local hearing at all. A conditional-use permit in a Utah county can need no neighbor notice whatsoever.

### Cited Findings
**Laws enacted that move notices off newspapers**
- **Florida (HB 7049, passed 2022, effective Jan 1 2023):** the first statewide law to drop the newspaper requirement, letting local governments in counties that run a public-notice website post notices there instead. [S, summary by the authors' school] — [Yale SOM Insights](https://insights.som.yale.edu/insights/removing-government-notices-from-newspapers-reduces-citizen-participation-in-decision); [S] working-paper abstract quoted by [PNRC Feb 2026 newsletter](https://www.pnrc.net/?p=4098); paper: [SSRN 6168629](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=6168629) (not opened directly)
  - The study (Munevar, Nakhmurina, Samuels; working paper, not peer reviewed) analyzed more than 150,000 notices from Oct 2020 to Dec 2024. In counties with a website, newspaper notices fell **37%** relative to other counties: **hearing notices 44%, zoning notices 27%**. County notice websites showed no significant traffic increase. Speakers per meeting fell **14–18%**. Commercial construction permits rose **28–35%**. Similar bills were introduced in 14 other states the prior year. [S] — [Yale SOM Insights](https://insights.som.yale.edu/insights/removing-government-notices-from-newspapers-reduces-citizen-participation-in-decision)
- **Ohio (HB 33, 2023):** lets municipalities publish many notices on their own websites and social media, or on the Ohio News Media Association's statewide site, instead of in newspapers. Townships were initially excluded. [S, PNRC is a newspaper-industry group] — [PNRC, Sept 2023](https://www.pnrc.net/2023/09/25/ohio-enacts-law-curtailing-newspaper-notice/)
  - The current Ohio township rezoning statute (ORC 519.12, page updated July 8 2026) allows hearing notice "by one publication at least ten days before" the hearing using **any one** of: a newspaper (print or digital), the official state public-notice website, **or "the web site and social media account of the township."** Mailed notice, at least 10 days ahead, goes only to owners "within and contiguous to and directly across the street from" the area, and only when 10 or fewer parcels are rezoned. The amendment history lists HB 33, HB 315, HB 96 and HB 479. [V] — [ORC 519.12](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-519.12)
  - 2025 amendments (HB 331/HB 497) let agencies replace repeat newspaper publications with a short notice that points to the state site. [S] — [ORC 125.182](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-125.182)
- **New Jersey (S4654, signed mid-2025, mandatory from March 1 2026):** public entities must publish legal notices on official government websites and on eligible online news publications, with a dedicated legal-notice page linked from each homepage and a central page run by the Secretary of State. The newspaper mandate is removed. [S] — [PNRC Aug 2025 newsletter](https://www.pnrc.net/2025/08/05/august-2025-monthly-newsletter/); [NJ Monitor, Jul 1 2025](https://newjerseymonitor.com/2025/07/01/new-jersey-removes-mandate-to-publish-legal-notices-in-newspapers/); [NJ League of Municipalities](https://www.njslom.org/m/newsflash/Home/Detail/3656)
- **Indiana (HB 1210, 2026):** makes newspaper publication optional. Governments may instead use a print or electronic newspaper, a "locality newspaper," or their own website. The bill passed, but its effective date moved from July 1 2026 to **July 1 2027**. A follow-on bill, HB 1283, would add a state notice website with push alerts. The 2025 attempt, HB 1312, was defeated 8–2 in a Senate committee. [S] — [PNRC Mar 2026](https://www.pnrc.net/2026/03/03/march-2026-monthly-newsletter/); [PNRC Feb 2026](https://www.pnrc.net/?p=4098); [PNRC Feb 2025](https://www.pnrc.net/2025/02/06/legislative-floodgates-open/)
- **Louisiana (HB 526, 2025):** creates a state-run website as the state's official journal for notices of state boards and commissions. Its final status was not confirmed. [S] — [PNRC Aug 2025](https://www.pnrc.net/2025/08/05/august-2025-monthly-newsletter/)

### Inferences
- The trend in notice law points one way. Where states loosen newspaper requirements, the legally sufficient notice moves to government websites that few people check. In Florida, those websites drew no extra traffic, and participation fell most for exactly the hearing and zoning notices 351 Watch flags. Monitoring agendas and notice pages fills that gap directly. Florida, Ohio townships, New Jersey (from March 2026) and Indiana (from July 2027) are natural places to pitch "we read the government websites so you don't have to."
- Even the strongest statutes (Massachusetts, Michigan) reach only owners within 300 ft. Virginia mails notice only 5 days ahead. A data center or solar project's real impact zone (noise, traffic, power lines) is far larger than the mailing radius, so most affected residents get no direct notice anywhere.
- The deepest gaps are in places where no hearing is required at all: Texas unincorporated land, Utah CUPs, and Georgia text amendments. In those places the only early signals are agenda items for tax abatements, road-use agreements, annexations, utility or air-permit notices, and economic-development board meetings. Agenda monitoring for those bodies, not just planning boards, is where the value lies.
- New York's S07053, if enacted, would let counties without a local newspaper post notices only online. That makes 351 Watch's existing New York coverage more valuable.

The pages were behind JavaScript or Cloudflare walls, so both remain unverified. Kentucky, Indiana, Mississippi and Tennessee land-use notice statutes were also not read.

- No national compendium of land-use notice radii and lead times was found. A 50-state table would need statute-by-statute work.

## 3. Where do news deserts and thin local news overlap with heavy siting or data-center activity? (Ranked)

### Takeaway
The strongest overlaps are in five places:
1. Georgia's exurban and rural counties: the most people in low-news counties, plus a wave of data-center moratoria.
2. Texas unincorporated counties: the most desert counties, no county zoning, and booming data-center and battery development.
3. Virginia's Piedmont and Northern Neck: news deserts doubled, and the state is the national data-center epicenter with the second-most contested renewable projects.
4. Ohio's townships and exurbs: top-five newspaper losses, website-only township notice, and county bans on renewables.
5. Rural Utah: about 79% of counties have one outlet or none, and mega data centers have been approved with no neighbor notice.

Next come Louisiana and the Mississippi Delta, Oklahoma's Tulsa ring, Indiana, North Carolina's northeast and Piedmont, New Mexico, and Kentucky.

## 4. How do residents and landowners currently learn about hearings, and is there evidence they miss them?

### Takeaway
Word of mouth and community Facebook or Nextdoor groups now reach more people than newspapers or government websites. Pew's December 2025 survey: 72% get local news from other residents and 52% from local online forums, versus 36% from a daily paper and 40% from local government. The best national evidence of missed hearings is Berkeley Lab's 2024 survey of solar-project neighbors: only 17% knew about the project before construction, and more than a third of people within 3 miles did not know it existed even after it was built. In data-center fights, people typically find out from a sign on a fence, a pipeline company at the door, an air-permit notice, a rumor or a Facebook post, often after approval.

### Cited Findings
- **Pew (survey Dec 8–14 2025; fact sheet Apr 14 2026):** share of US adults who often or sometimes get local news from each source:
  - Other local residents (friends, family, neighbors), 72%, up from 66% in 2018. Pew's 2024 work found this is mostly word of mouth.
  - Local online forums or discussion groups "such as community Facebook groups or Nextdoor," 52%, up from 38% in 2018.
  - Local government agencies or officials, 40%, up from 30% in 2018.
  - Community newsletters or listservs, 37%.
  - Their local daily paper, 36%, down from 43% in 2018.
  - Local TV, 65%.

  Only 21% follow local news "very closely," down from 37% in 2016. [V] for the forums, residents, government and newsletter figures (page read directly) — [Pew Local News Fact Sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2026/04/14/local-news-fact-sheet/). [S] for the daily paper (36%), local TV (65%) and "very closely" (21% vs 37%) figures, which come from a search summary of the same page
- **Berkeley Lab, University of Michigan and Michigan State (survey 2023, published Apr 11 2024):**
  - 984 responses from people living within 3 miles of large-scale solar projects; 90% were mail surveys.

  - **More than a third** of the population within 3 miles was not aware of the local solar project at all. More than a third of respondents near projects under 100 MW did not know the project existed.

### Inferences
- The real distribution channels are Facebook groups, neighbors and advocacy-group alerts. They activate *after* a visible trigger such as a fence sign, pipeline survey, rumor or press release, which is often after the vote. 351 Watch's lead time (reading agendas days ahead) is valuable exactly here. Its feeds (RSS, calendar, Slack or Teams) would be most useful as feeds into the community Facebook groups, local advocacy groups and county officials who already relay information.
- Press-association notice sites exist almost everywhere, but they are keyword-search archives of legal ads. They do not classify siting items or push alerts. Where governments leave newspapers (Florida, New Jersey, Ohio townships, Indiana from 2027), those archives lose coverage.
- Agenda monitoring will not catch everything. NDA-stage deals and agendas that obscure the project (Claremore) mean the system should also watch economic-development boards, utility and co-op boards, annexations, tax-abatement items and state air or water permits.

- I did not retrieve the LBNL/Michigan surveys of local officials about renewable siting (the MPPS energy modules), so officials' own views on notice adequacy are not captured.
- I found no quantitative data on Facebook group reach for siting fights.

## 5. Concrete examples of harm from late discovery, on both sides

### Takeaway
On the resident side, the pattern is approval first and discovery later: Juab County UT, Sand Springs OK, Boyce LA, Box Elder UT and solar neighbors nationally. On the developer side, the notice gap cuts the other way: approvals voided for defective notice (Prince William's Digital Gateway, roughly 2,100 acres and 37 buildings, now dead) and moratoria or zoning changes adopted quickly that strand land contracts (Hill County TX, Peculiar MO, Vance County NC). Both sides lose when information arrives late.

### Cited Findings
**Residents learning after approval [S unless noted]**

- **Kerrville TX:** new setback and screening rules (500 ft from homes and schools) "effectively block data centers [and] battery storage without an outright ban." — [Kerr County Lead](https://kerrcountylead.com/kerrvilles-new-rules-effectively-block-data-centers-battery-storage-without-an-outright-ban/)
- **Renewables, at scale:** about 40% of 567 contested projects have been canceled. 22 cancellations in 2025 totaled about 9.8 GW. 30 Ohio counties ban development under SB 52. [V/S] — [Sabin blog](https://blogs.law.columbia.edu/climatechange/2026/09/03/widespread-local-opposition-often-driven-by-misinformation-continues-to-hamper-renewable-energy-development/); [pv magazine](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/09/08/the-u-s-saw-a-14-percent-increase-in-contested-renewable-projects-9-8-gw-in-cancellations-in-2025/)
- **Public notice and newspaper economics:** the Aurelia Star in Iowa closed after the county stopped placing notices in it. [V] — [Medill 2025 PDF](https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/assets/slnp/the_state_of_local_news_2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- Developers face a two-sided risk in information-poor places. Residents who find out late organize fast and push for moratoria. Coverage of Data Center Watch's 2026 reports says communities are pursuing moratoria "sometimes before developers can submit permit applications" ([S], search summary of [Data Center Watch coverage](https://www.datacenterwatch.org/report)). Counties that act fast can overstep their authority (Hill), and notice defects can void approvals (Digital Gateway). An early, neutral agenda feed shortens that reaction cycle for everyone, which is a credible pitch to land agents, project developers and county attorneys as well as residents.
- The examples cluster in the same ranked regions: Texas, Utah, Virginia, Georgia, Oklahoma, Louisiana and the Carolinas.

### Gaps
- I did not find a solar or wind example in 2025–2026 of a developer suing over a moratorium adopted with little notice. Search budget ran out. Data-center examples dominate the developer-side list.
- The final appellate details and dates for the Digital Gateway case and the Lowell Township, Pine Island and Socorro items come from search summaries and should be confirmed from the primary articles.

## 6. Which local civic-information projects exist, and where are they absent?

### Takeaway
City Bureau's Documenters network lists 30 sites; 6 are "in hibernation" and nearly all are metro areas. The only rural or small-region sites are Nebraska Panhandle, Columbia Gorge, Centre County PA and Doña Ana NM. Press-association notice websites exist in 45 states but are passive archives. Nonprofit and digital newsrooms are concentrated in metro areas. The ranked target regions (rural Georgia, rural Texas, the Virginia Piedmont, Ohio townships, rural Utah, Louisiana, the Mississippi Delta, northeastern North Carolina) have essentially no meeting-monitoring service.

### Inferences
- Documenters' absence maps closely onto the ranked target regions. In Georgia only Atlanta has a site, Texas only Dallas and Fort Worth, Ohio only its big cities, North Carolina only Charlotte. Virginia, Utah, Louisiana and Mississippi have none. A software-only agenda monitor fills the gap Documenters leaves, rural county commissions and township boards, and could partner with Documenters by giving their metro sites a regional feed.
- Press-association notice sites are both potential partners and competitors. They cover legal ads but not agendas, and they are losing inventory where states let governments self-publish.
- Rhode Island has no listed statewide press-association notice site. That supports 351 Watch's existing RI portal as filling a real gap.
