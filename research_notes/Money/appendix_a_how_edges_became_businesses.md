# How public-record edges became businesses, and what that teaches a town-agenda feed

Written 2026-10-26 for the owner of Agenda Ahead. Every number below is linked to the page it came from. Where a figure is a third-party estimate or a single unconfirmed source, it says so. Where I could not find a number (Curate's price list, Glenigan's prices, GovWin's price, a documented rezoning land-flipper), it says so rather than guessing.

The short version: nobody on this list got rich from the record itself. The record was free. They got paid for the labor of reading it first, normalizing it, keeping the history, and putting it in front of the one buyer who loses money by being late.

---

## Cases

### 1. CoStar (1987 → $3.25B revenue; ~$40B peak market cap)

- **The record.** Andrew Florance began in 1986–87 by hand: he "called brokers," "pulled public city filings by hand" and "surveyed buildings in person," seeding a database with Dun & Bradstreet and government economic-development publications, then had researchers phone brokers for current availabilities ([Collision](https://collision.substack.com/p/foundations-costar-the-company-that), [Reference for Business](https://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/13/CoStar-Group-Inc.html)).
- **First product and price.** His first firm, Real Estate Infonet, printed ten copies of ~1,200 pages of compiled public records and sold each for **$800**. Then came Realty Information Group and a paper leasing guide, *Cornerstone* (1989), sold off in 1993 ([Reference for Business](https://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/13/CoStar-Group-Inc.html)).
- **Who paid.** Washington, D.C. brokers who outsourced research after the late-1980s crash. Expansion: Baltimore and New York in 1994, then LA, Chicago, Philadelphia, SF ([Collision](https://collision.substack.com/p/foundations-costar-the-company-that)).
- **How long.** 1989: $30,000 revenue against $1M of expenses. 1990: "well more than $500,000." 1997: $7.9M. IPO July 1998 raised ~$22.5–23M; accumulated deficit at filing $11.4M ([Reference for Business](https://www.referenceforbusiness.com/history2/13/CoStar-Group-Inc.html), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CoStar_Group)). Market cap: $111M (1998), $1.2B (2010), $36.4B (2020), peak ~$40B (July 2025), **$13.7B on Oct 23, 2026** after the Homes.com spending fight ([FinHacker](https://www.finhacker.cz/en/stocks/costar-group-market-cap/)).
- **Scale.** 2025 revenue $3.25B, 8,400 employees; acquisitions LoopNet $860M (2012), Apartments.com $585M (2014), Matterport $1.6B, Domain $1.92B, Zonda $800M (2026) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CoStar_Group)). Subscriptions are quote-based; third-party estimates put a seat at roughly $200–500+/month ([ThePricer](https://www.thepricer.org/how-much-does-costar-membership-cost), low-authority).
- **Moat defense.** CoStar sued Xceligent (judgment $500M, see counter-examples), CREXi, and Zillow over copied photos ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CoStar_Group)).
- **Lesson.** Ten years from the $800 binder to $8M of revenue. The product was never "access to records"; it was a verified, photographed, continuously re-called database that each year got harder to copy, plus lawyers.

### 2. F.W. Dodge / Dodge Construction Network and ConstructConnect (1891 → $320M and $631M sales)

- **The record.** Frederick W. Dodge started in 1891 gathering construction-project data around Boston on index cards, tracking building-permit activity in the Northeast ([Dodge history](https://www.construction.com/about/), [MarketScale](https://www.marketscale.com/industries/engineering-and-construction/dodge-construction-network-how-130-years-of-data-is-reshaping-construction-intelligence)).
- **Who pays.** Subcontractors and suppliers who need to know what is about to bid. McGraw-Hill owned it from 1961.
- **Prices paid for the business.** McGraw Hill Financial sold McGraw Hill Construction (Dodge, ENR, Architectural Record, Sweets) to Symphony Technology Group for **$320M cash**, closing Nov 3, 2014 ([S&P press release](https://press.spglobal.com/2014-09-22-McGraw-Hill-Financial-to-Sell-McGraw-Hill-Construction-to-Symphony-Technology-Group)). Clearlake became an equal partner in Jan 2022; the company then claimed ~40,000 customers and 1M+ registered users ([Dodge](https://www.construction.com/clearlake-capital-group-to-invest-in-dodge-construction-network/)). In Nov 2024 it needed $100M of new capital and a new CEO ([CBO](https://www.constructionbusinessowner.com/news/dodge-construction-network-announces-executive-leadership-changes-100m-new-capital)).
- **ConstructConnect** (iSqFt + BidClerk + CMD): Roper paid **$631M cash** on Oct 31, 2016 for a business expected to add ~$150M of revenue, about 4.2x ([Roper 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000882835/000088283516000052/q3-16_10q.htm), [Business Observer](https://www.businessobserverfl.com/news/2016/nov/02/firm-constructs-deal-add-150m-revenues/)).
- **Lesson.** "Who is about to build what" has been a paid product for 135 years and sells at 3–4x revenue to private equity. It is also a mature, slow-growth category: the 2024 recapitalization shows the toll road can need repaving.

### 3. Curate (Madison, WI; 2016 → eight-figure sale in 2021)

- **The record.** Municipal meeting agendas and minutes. Dale Willis (a UW–Madison CS PhD student) and Taralinda Willis (who had fielded late-bidding contractors on a $100M campus project) founded Curate in May 2016; the software scanned 7,000+ Wisconsin agendas/minutes, pulled keywords and sent contractors a **weekly report** of projects before the RFP ([UW–Madison](https://news.wisc.edu/uw-madison-spinoff-gives-builders-a-head-start-on-new-projects), [BizTimes](https://biztimes.com/idea-fund-makes-first-startup-investment/)).
- **Who paid.** Mid-to-large commercial and education contractors, later associations and building-product firms; 125+ businesses in 21 states by 2019 ([Xconomy](https://legacy.xconomy.com/?p=363100)). Pitch: "reliable data, timeliness, understanding the market." Output was "an Excel-type format; we don't send out paragraphs" ([UW–Madison](https://news.wisc.edu/uw-madison-spinoff-gives-builders-a-head-start-on-new-projects)). Price list: not disclosed anywhere I could find.
- **Money and time.** $450K seed led by the Idea Fund of La Crosse; $1.65M seed-plus with Allos Ventures ([BizTimes](https://biztimes.com/curate-solutions-closes-1-6m-funding-round/)). Team: 4 → 9 (2018) → 24–25 at sale. Coverage: 72 Wisconsin counties → 12,000+ U.S. municipalities with 2,000+ people ([Wisconsin Technology Council](https://wisconsintechnologycouncil.com/?p=59375), [FiscalNote](https://fiscalnote.com/newsroom/fiscalnote-announces-acquisition-of-curate)).
- **Exit.** FiscalNote approached them and closed Aug 31, 2021; terms undisclosed, described as **"eight-figure"** by Badger Fund partner Ken Johnson; Willis said the dataset was the main draw ([Wisconsin Technology Council](https://wisconsintechnologycouncil.com/?p=59375), [gener8tor](https://gener8tor.com/news/gener8tor-alumni-curate-solutions-bought-by-washington-dc-firm)).
- **Lesson.** This is the closest analogue to Agenda Ahead. One state, one buyer type (contractors who lose bids by being late), a weekly spreadsheet, five years, ~$2M raised, sold for $10M+ to a buyer who wanted the archive, not the scraper.

### 4. Glenigan (UK planning applications; 1973 → £72.9M sale)

- **The record.** Local-authority planning applications. In 1973 field researchers collected them by hand at councils and mailed them to Bournemouth; staff transcribed, photocopied and posted *Sales Leads*, ~180 projects a week. 1982: first database, 800 projects/week. 1985: researchers got laptops. 2002: first online service. 2005: live capture from council planning portals ([Glenigan history](https://www.glenigan.com/history-of-glenigan/)).
- **Who pays.** Contractors, subcontractors, and product manufacturers; pricing is quote-only ("tiered," depends on users and modules) ([Glenigan pricing](https://www.glenigan.com/?p=44)).
- **Exit.** Ascential sold Glenigan to Byggfakta for **£72.9M cash**, agreed Dec 2020 ([Media Mergers](https://www.mediamergers.co.uk/ascential-has-entered-into-an-agreement-to-sell-glenigan-to-byggfakta-group-for-72-9m/)). Site shows ~594,000 live projects ([Glenigan](https://www.glenigan.com/about-us/)).
- **Moat move.** In 2025 Glenigan signed an exclusive partnership with Planning Portal to receive applications **three weeks before they appear on council websites** ([Glenigan history](https://www.glenigan.com/history-of-glenigan/)).
- **Lesson.** Fifty years in business and the moat kept shifting: from field labor, to a database, to live scraping, to exclusive upstream access. Whoever gets the document first wins, and that is a relationship, not code.

### 5. Reonomy, BuildZoom/BuildFax, Zonda, Placer.ai, Shovels (property-record startups, 2012–2026)

- **Reonomy** (2013): built a 50M+ commercial-property dataset from tax parcels, deeds and contacts; ~115 staff; sold to Altus Group for **$201.5M** (Nov 2021) ([Altus](https://www.altusgroup.com/press-releases/altus-group-acquires-reonomy/)). Today $500/month or $400/month annual ([Reonomy](https://reonomy.com/pricing)).
- **BuildZoom** (YC W13): permit and license records from 2,000+ building departments; $1.4M seed (2013); charges contractors a **2.5% commission** on jobs won ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/buildzoom-yc-w13-raises-1-dollars-dot-4m-from-formation-8-and-angels-has-over-3m-licensed-building-contractor-listings/), [SF Chronicle](https://preview-prod.w.sfchronicle.com/business/article/BuildZoom-bids-for-contractors-time-4-more-11177234.php)). **BuildFax**: permit data sold to insurers; Verisk paid **$40.4M** net (Oct 2019) ([Verisk 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001442145/000144214520000012/vrsk10k12312019.htm)).
- **Zonda**: Meyers Research (2006) merged with Hanley Wood/Metrostudy under MidOcean in Dec 2018, rebranded 2020 ([Builder](https://builderonline.com/money/m-a/midocean-partners-acquires-hanley-wood-and-meyers-research_o)); ~$170M revenue at 23% EBITDA (2025); CoStar paid **$800M cash** (announced May 29, 2026; closed Aug 2026), ~4.7x revenue ([Pulse2](https://pulse2.com/costar-buying-zonda-in-800-million-deal/amp/), [Inman](https://www.inman.com/2026/05/29/costar-acquire-zonda-800m/), [Online Marketplaces](https://www.onlinemarketplaces.com/articles/costar-group-closes-800m-zonda-acquisition/)).
- **Placer.ai** (2018; phone location data, not public records, included as a pricing/scale comparator): ~$30M ARR (Jan 2022) → $100M+ (Feb 2024), 1,000 → 4,300 customers, $1.5B valuation ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/08/05/placer-ai-boosts-valuation-to-1-5b-after-quietly-raising-another-75m/)).
- **Shovels** (2022): cleans permit records from 20,000 jurisdictions; $5M seed June 2025; ~$250K MRR (~$3M ARR) as of Aug 2026 per Sacra, unverified ([Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/?p=523692), [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/shovels/)).
- **Lesson.** Raw permit data alone fetched ~$40M (BuildFax). The $200M–$800M exits layered ownership, contacts, and a known buyer population on top, and sold to an adjacent data giant.

### 6. GovSpend, BidNet, GovWin/INPUT (procurement intelligence)

- **GovSpend / SmartProcure** (Deerfield Beach FL, 2011): sends recurring public-records requests to agencies for line-item purchase orders; claims nearly 1B records from ~30,000 agencies; vendors pay subscriptions for competitive intel and agency outreach, governments use it for price benchmarking. Thompson Street Capital bought it Jan 2021 (undisclosed); Endicott joined; it bought Fedmine Aug 2021 ([TSCP](https://www.tscp.com/news/thompson-street-capital-partners-announces-the-acquisition-of-govspend/), [Preqin](https://preqin.com/data/profile/asset/smartprocure-inc-/408209)). Cost of the method: librarians on a Michigan list traded notes on quarterly SmartProcure FOIAs, attorney advice that an agency may deny if records aren't kept in the requested form, and one library said after that "the company left us alone" ([michlib-l](https://lists.mcls.org/pipermail/michlib-l/2018-July/014765.html)).
- **BidNet** (Latham NY, 1983): aggregates bid notices from thousands of agencies; now a unit of mdf commerce, which KKR took private for ~C$255M in 2024 ([Craft](https://craft.co/bidnet), [BetaKit](https://betakit.com/mdf-commerce-to-go-private-in-255-million-acquisition-by-new-york-based-kkr/)).
- **INPUT → GovWin**: Deltek paid **$60M cash** (Oct 2010) for INPUT, which had $26.2M revenue and ~200 staff (~2.3x) ([Washington Technology](https://www.washingtontechnology.com/2010/09/deltek-makes-60m-deal-for-input/348690/)); Deltek itself sold to Thoma Bravo for $1.1B in 2012 ([FedScoop](https://fedscoop.com/deltek-sells-to-private-firm-for-1-1-billion/)). GovWin prices are not public.
- **Lesson.** "What is government about to buy" is a decades-old paid feed with repeat PE buyers. FOIA at scale works but it is a cost center and a relationship problem with clerks; the companies that last make the requests routine and painless for the agency.

### 7. FiscalNote, Quorum, Politico Pro, Punchbowl (legislative intelligence by the seat)

- **Politico Pro** launched Feb 2011 at **$3,295/year** per individual per vertical; group plans (~five people, one vertical) around **$8,000**; "well over 95 percent" organizational ([Nieman Lab 2012](https://www.niemanlab.org/2012/04/politico-pro-one-year-in-a-premium-pricetag-a-tight-focus-and-a-business-success/)). By March 2013: 1,000 organizations, 7,000 people, **96% renewal** ([Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/encyclo/politico)). By 2015 subscriptions started ~$10,000 and Pro was **half of Politico's revenue** ([Folio](https://read.nxtbook.com/folio/foliomag/junejuly2015/howpoliticoprofited_casestudy.html)); U.S. deals ran $10,000–$300,000 ([Press Gazette](https://pressgazette.co.uk/archive-content/politico-introduces-paid-professional-subscription-services-europe-five-months-after-launch/)). Axel Springer bought Politico for more than $1B in 2021 ([Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2021/08/politico-is-being-sold-for-more-than-1-billion-here-are-some-of-the-smart-moves-it-made-to-get-there/)).
- **Quorum** (2014, Harvard dorm): no outside money, seeded by business-plan competitions and grants; **$4,800/year per user**, shareable across an organization; profitable in 2015 ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/washington-dc/tech/what-you-didnt-know-about-dc-techs-newest-member-46479), [Albuquerque Journal](https://www.pressreader.com/usa/albuquerque-journal/20150202/282269548811415)); ~2,000 customers by 2024 after buying Capitol Canary and CisionPAC ([Quorum](https://www.quorum.us/company-news/celebrates-10-years-innovation)).
- **Punchbowl News** (Jan 3, 2021): **$10M revenue in year one, profitable**, with nearly 90% from sponsorships; premium tier **$300/year**; ~$20M revenue 2023 ([Rebooting](https://therebooting.substack.com/p/jake-sherman-on-punchbowls-10m-first), [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2023/09/26/punchbowl-news-expands-website-20m-revenue), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punchbowl_News)). A critic counted only ~3,000 paying readers ([Media Roundup](https://themediaroundup.beehiiv.com/p/thursday-25th-november-please-make-clear-readers-subscriber)).
- **FiscalNote** (2013): went public via SPAC in 2022 at an implied ~$1.3B, forecasting $173M of 2022 revenue, 90% subscriptions ([S-4](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1823466/000110465922046913/tm2132074-36_s4a.htm), [Flashes & Flames](https://flashesandflames.com/2021/11/11/how-the-economist-missed-out/)). Actual 2024 revenue **$120.3M, down from $132.6M**; it sold Board.org and Aicel; got NYSE sub-$1 notices in Nov 2024 and Apr 2025 ([10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1823466/000095017025048724/note-20241231.htm), [8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1823466/000095017025053292/note-20250410.htm)).
- **Lesson.** Price per seat per topic, sold to organizations, and live on renewal rate. Sponsorship can carry year one (Punchbowl). Buying a dozen datasets and calling it a platform (FiscalNote) did not.

### 8. Thinknum, Orbital Insight, RS Metrics (alternative data sold to investors)

- **RS Metrics**: brothers Tom and Alex Diamond (ex-DigitalGlobe) began selling satellite parking-lot counts; their 2010 break was a UBS analyst buying 100 Walmart lot counts ([Trusted Insight](https://www.thetrustedinsight.com/investment-news/how-investors-use-satellite-imagery-and-alternative-data-20190416232/), [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/amp/id/38722872)). Berkeley researchers using 4.8M images of 67,000 stores found the edge was worth **4–5% in the three days around earnings**, and that although available since 2011 it "hasn't spread beyond a select few large investors" ([Berkeley Haas](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/how-hedge-funds-use-satellite-images-to-beat-wall-street-and-main-street/)).
- **Thinknum** (2014): web-scraped job listings, store counts, app ranks; list price **$16,800/year** flat ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/204405/Thinknum/)); profitable since 2016; $11.6M Series A ([Hedgeweek](https://www.hedgeweek.com/alt-data-provider-thinknum-secures-usd116-million-series-funding-round/)).
- **Orbital Insight** (2013): raised ~$125–132M; sold to Privateer in spring 2024 for an undisclosed sum, announced alongside Privateer's own $56.5M raise ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_Insight), [CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/orbital-insight/financials), [Pulse2](https://pulse2.com/privateer-raises-56-5-million-and-acquires-orbital-insight/amp/)).
- **Lesson.** An edge sold to traders is priced by alpha and decays as it spreads. The bootstrapped, flat-priced shop (Thinknum) stayed profitable; the one that raised $130M to out-compute the field sold quietly.

### 9. Panjiva and ImportGenius (customs bills of lading)

- **ImportGenius** (2007; Ryan and David Petersen, Michael Kanko): organized every U.S. inbound shipping manifest, obtained via customs reporting rules and FOIA, into a searchable, daily-updated database; **$99/month standard, $250 premium** in 2008; from $125/month today ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2008/05/28/importgenius-the-disruptive-shipping-database/amp/), [Freakonomics](https://freakonomics.com/2008/06/amazing-new-trade-data), [ImportGenius](https://w3.importgenius.com/comparison/trade-data-monitor)). Ryan Petersen went on to found Flexport in 2013 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryan_Petersen_(businessman))).
- **Panjiva** (2006; Josh Green and James Psota, after Green couldn't find a reliable Chinese supplier at E Ink): FOIA'd U.S. manifests plus D&B/Kompass; raised VC in 2008; S&P Global bought it Feb 2018, undisclosed ([FreightWaves](https://www.freightwaves.com/news/panjiva-ceo-highlights-companys-evolution-around-big-data), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panjiva), [Oaklins](https://www.oaklins.com/us/en/deals/103804/)).
- **Erosion.** Under [19 CFR 103.31](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/103.31) any importer can certify its name and its shippers' names confidential, and compliance circulars tell importers to do exactly that because the data "is often sold in the marketplace in publications like Piers or Panjiva" ([Ascent](https://ascentlogistics.com/?p=233)).
- **Lesson.** Same free record, two businesses: a cheap self-serve subscription still independent after 19 years, and an enterprise product acquired by S&P. Both live with a legal opt-out that slowly thins the feed.

### 10. PitchBook (2007 → $671.8M revenue)

- John Gabbert (ex-VentureOne) founded it in 2007 at the start of the recession; by 2014 it had raised only $4.25M and subscriptions averaged **$20,000/year** ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2014/venture-capital-data-provider-pitchbook-says-theyre-sale/)).
- Mid-2016: 1,800 customers, **$31.1M trailing revenue**, 300+ staff; Morningstar paid ~$180M for the 80% it didn't own, valuing it at **$225M** (~7x revenue) ([Finextra](https://www.finextra.com/pressarticle/66586/morningstar-to-acquire-pitchbook?)).
- 2025 revenue **$671.8M**, +8.6%, adjusted operating margin 31.3%; licensed users flat as corporate churn offset new logos ([Morningstar](https://newsroom.morningstar.com/news/news-details/2026/Morningstar-Inc--Reports-Fourth-Quarter-Full-Year-2025-Financial-Results/default.aspx)).
- **Lesson.** Nine years to $31M, then 20x in nine more under an owner with distribution. The data is assembled from filings, press and phone calls, the same labor as CoStar.

### 11. Bloomberg (1981 → ~$15B revenue)

- Michael Bloomberg started with a $10M Salomon payout in 1981. **Merrill Lynch was the first customer: 20 (some sources say 22) terminals plus $30M for 30%**, with a five-year exclusivity Merrill waived in 1984 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomberg_L.P.), [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/bloomberg-terminal)).
- Terminal: ~$24,000/user/year (Wikipedia); third-party estimates put 2025 list at $31,980 single / $28,320 multi-seat after a 6.5% increase ([NeuGroup](https://connect.neugroup.com/public/blogs/bloomberg-terminals-how-much-more-youll-pay-next-year)). Subscribers 325,301 in 2017 per Burton-Taylor ([Hedge Fund Alpha](https://hedgefundalpha.com/news/ft-trolls-bloomberg/)). Revenue ~$10B (2018, leaked memo) and ~$15B (2024); terminal was >85% of revenue in 2011. Merrill's last 20% sold back in 2008 for $4.43B, valuing the firm at ~$22.5B ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloomberg_L.P.)).
- **Lesson.** One anchor customer paid for the build. No discounting, ever. The moat became the users themselves (messaging, contributed prices), not the public bond data it began with.

### 12. LexisNexis CourtLink (1989 → sold 2001)

- Bellevue, WA company that put dockets online across all U.S. district and bankruptcy courts, 11 of 13 appeals courts, and many state courts; LexisNexis agreed to buy it Oct 31, 2001, price undisclosed and rumored "well below" the $95M Kluwer paid for Loislaw ([Nextgov](https://nextgov.com/digital-government/2001/11/lexisnexis-to-buy-courtlink/217685), [Law.com](https://www.law.com/almID/900005526425/)).
- **Lesson.** Stitching many courthouses into one search was worth tens of millions before PACER made the raw dockets cheap. The free government portal caps what the raw feed can charge; the value moved to alerts and analytics.

### 13. Small operators living on a narrow public feed

- **Capitol Fax** (Illinois, 1993). Rich Miller, "founder, publisher, editor and sole reporter," sells a daily Statehouse newsletter at $350/year (2010) and ~$500/year; fewer than 2,000 subscribers ([NPR Illinois](https://www.nprillinois.org/statehouse/2010-02-01/about-rich-miller-newsletter-came-long-before-blogs-yet-includes-analysis-opinion-advice), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitol_Fax)). State agencies alone paid his company ~$38,400 (2023) and ~$38,800 (2022); public employees spent $250,000+ on it in 2005–2013 ([Edgar County Watchdogs](https://edgarcountywatchdogs.com/?p=87227), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitol_Fax)).
- **Chicago Cityscape** (2014). Urban planner Steven Vance sent his first paid newsletter on May 11, 2014 **to two people at $40/year**; 35 paid subscribers after a year; the free list now exceeds 7,000. It became a permits-and-zoning platform (Permit Tracker, Lead Finder for proposed projects and zoning changes) with Real Estate Pro and custom Enterprise tiers and an API at $1,000/year ([Cityscape turns 6](https://www.chicagocityscape.com/blog/chicago-cityscape-turns-6-8a60159950), [About](https://www.chicagocityscape.com/about.php), [Pricing](https://www.chicagocityscape.com/pricing.php)).
- **Construction Monitor** (Parowan then Cedar City, Utah, 1989). Began as a mailed newsletter of building permits; now ~20,000 permit leads a week across ~70 U.S. areas, sold to suppliers and subcontractors; ~50 staff plus 150 field contributors in 2018 ([History](https://constructionmonitor.com/aboutus/history), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Construction_Monitor), [Blog](https://blog.constructionmonitor.com/about/)).
- **The Warren Group / Banker & Tradesman** (Boston, 1872). Started as "a simple compendium of recent real estate transactions"; bought the *Transfer Directory* in 1986, launched Real Estate Records Search in 1998, went national with data in 2019; fifth generation of the family joined the board in 2021 ([Warren Group history](https://www.thewarrengroup.com/history-of-the-warren-group/), [B&T 150th](https://bankerandtradesman.com/industry-news/150-anniversary/page/3)).
- **What Now Media Group** (Atlanta, 2010 → 28–30 cities). Caleb Spivak's openings-and-closings sites, built on permits and license filings and sold to vendors as "upcoming restaurant openings up to 6 months before anyone else" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caleb_Spivak), [What Now](https://whatnow.com/portland/real-estate/what-now-media-group-expands-reach-with-launch-of-what-now-portland), [About](https://whatnow.com/about/)).
- **Universal Hub and Boston Restaurant Talk** (Boston). Adam Gaffin's one-person site (2005) runs a standing Boston Licensing Board feed and ZBA items, funded by reader contributions, ads, and consulting ([Boston Magazine](https://www.bostonmagazine.com/?p=2412016), [UH licensing tag](https://images.universalhub.com/boston-licensing?page=3)); Marc Hurwitz's Boston Restaurant Talk breaks openings from Licensing Board hearing dockets ([NBC Boston](https://www.nbcboston.com/entertainment/food-drink-nightlife/seeing-which-boston-restaurants-dont-reopen-in-phase-2-will-be-scary-insider-says/2135660/?amp=1)).
- **Data-center site scouts.** datacenterHawk (Dallas, 2014, ex-CBRE broker David Liggitt) sells subscriptions on supply/demand by market; one source reports it was acquired by Simplify Compliance in 2022 ([Data Center Map](https://www.datacentermap.com/blog/datacenterhawk)). Data Center Watch (a 10a Labs project) read local records to tally **$18B blocked and $46B delayed** by local opposition through March 2025, and the report ran on NBC and in the trade press ([DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/group-claims-64bn-in-us-data-center-projects-impacted-by-local-opposition-in-last-two-years/), [NBC](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/economics/state-local-opposition-new-data-centers-gaining-steam-rcna243838), [Data Center Watch](https://www.datacenterwatch.org)).
- **Not found.** I could not locate a documented, named land investor who makes a living reading rezoning notices; the only sources were cautionary (a Long Island family lost land to a rezoning nobody read: "Nobody reads them," said their lawyer, [CBS New York](https://www.cbsnews.com/newyork/news/long-island-rezoning-battle/)). The opportunity is real but the operators keep quiet.

## Counter-examples: edges that evaporated, and legal lines

- **Xceligent (CoStar's challenger).** Backed by Daily Mail (DMGT), it filed Chapter 7 in Dec 2017, shut its site and laid off hundreds; DMGT blamed weak NYC revenue and low renewals. CoStar had sued over photos copied by offshore contractors; the trustee agreed to a **$500M** judgment, with insurers reportedly liable for only ~$11M ([WealthManagement](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/financial-technology/what-xceligent-s-bankruptcy-says-about-the-property-data-business), [Bisnow](https://bisnow.com/national/news/technology/costar-wins-500m-judgment-in-xceligent-copyright-infringement-case-101421), [CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/1618004866/bisnow-ordered-to-return-200000-in-improper-payments-from-xceligent)). Line crossed: copying a competitor's compiled database and photos is not "public records."
- **hiQ v. LinkedIn.** hiQ scraped public profiles. It won the headline CFAA point in the Ninth Circuit (2019, reaffirmed 2022), then lost on **breach of LinkedIn's user agreement**, paid $500,000, destroyed the data under a permanent injunction, and is defunct ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HiQ_Labs_v._LinkedIn), [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/380493/web-scraper-agrees-to-pay-linkedin-500000-destr.html)). Line: a site's terms of service can beat "it was public."
- **Clearview AI.** Scraped 30B+ public photos into a face database: fined **£7.5M** (UK), **€20M** (Italy), **€30.5M** (Netherlands), and settled an Illinois biometric-privacy suit by restricting sales ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/uk-fines-clearview-ai-75-million-for-illegally-scraping-images-105018980.html), [MIT Tech Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2022/05/24/1052653/clearview-ai-data-privacy-uk), [Jurist](https://www.jurist.org/news/2024/09/netherlands-data-protection-authority-fines-us-ai-company-30-5-million-euros-over-facial-recognition-database), [Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2022/05/09/business/clearview-ai-settles-suit-agrees-limit-sales-facial-recognition-database/)). Line: public does not mean personal data about individuals is fair game.
- **Blaszczak (political intelligence).** A former CMS employee turned consultant passed pre-decisional Medicare rate information to hedge-fund traders; convicted in 2018 on Title 18 fraud counts, the convictions were vacated in Dec 2022 after the government conceded the agency information was not "property" post-*Kelly* ([MoFo](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/230123-second-circuit-nixes-theory), [BakerHostetler](https://www.bakerlaw.com/insights/second-circuit-vacates-insider-trading-convictions-narrowing-the-scope-of-future-prosecutions/)). Line: reading what a board posts is fine; getting what a staffer hasn't posted yet, for a trader, bought four years in criminal court even when it ended in acquittal.
- **EDGAR and the end of a resale business.** In 1993 Carl Malamud bought SEC filings and posted them free; when he took them down a year later, 17,000+ users demanded free access and the SEC put EDGAR online itself ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2012-04-12/one-mans-quest-to-make-information-free), [EFF 1993](https://w2.eff.org/effector/HTML/effect06.04.html)). Line: when the government publishes the record well, resellers of the raw record die; only synthesis survives.
- **Manifest confidentiality.** Importers can hide their names from the customs feed ImportGenius and Panjiva sell ([19 CFR 103.31](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/103.31)). The feed thins as the subjects learn it exists.
- **Orbital Insight.** $125M+ raised to sell satellite analytics to funds; sold in 2024 in a deal small enough to be bundled with the buyer's $56.5M raise ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/orbital-insight/financials), [Pulse2](https://pulse2.com/privateer-raises-56-5-million-and-acquires-orbital-insight/amp/)). The imagery got cheap; the edge went to zero.
- **FiscalNote.** $1.3B SPAC on a $173M forecast; $120M actual revenue two years later and two sub-$1 listing notices ([S-4](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1823466/000110465922046913/tm2132074-36_s4a.htm), [10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1823466/000095017025048724/note-20241231.htm)). Buying datasets is not the same as having customers who renew.
- **FOIA friction.** SmartProcure's blanket requests drew "commercial request" slow-walks, deposit demands, and denials; some agencies simply stopped responding ([michlib-l](https://lists.mcls.org/pipermail/michlib-l/2018-July/014765.html)). Public is a right; fast is a relationship.

## Patterns

1. **The record was always free; the product was the labor and the archive.** Dodge's index cards, Glenigan's field researchers, Florance's phone calls, Curate's parsers, Vance's Permit Tracker. Buyers pay for hours saved and for history they can query, not for access.
2. **The first ten customers were people who lose money by being late**, and who were already paying someone to read: D.C. brokers after the 1989 crash, Wisconsin contractors bidding late, UK subcontractors, lobbyists, a UBS analyst, Merrill Lynch. Nobody started with the general public.
3. **Pricing is per seat, per topic or geography, sold to organizations.** Politico Pro $3,295 → ~$10,000; Quorum $4,800; Thinknum $16,800; PitchBook ~$20,000; Bloomberg $24,000–32,000. Cheap individual tiers exist and work for one-person shops (Capitol Fax $500/yr, Cityscape $40/yr at launch, ImportGenius $99–125/mo), but the organizations pay the bills, and >95% of Politico Pro's buyers were organizations.
4. **Renewal rate is the business.** Politico Pro 96%; Punchbowl 90% paid retention; PitchBook's "flat licenses" line was the one soft spot in a $672M year; Xceligent died of "lower renewal rates."
5. **Real money took 5–10 years; exits priced at 2–7x revenue.** CoStar: 10 years to $8M. PitchBook: 9 years to $31M, sold at 7x. Curate: 5 years to eight figures. INPUT 2.3x, ConstructConnect 4.2x, Zonda 4.7x, BuildFax ~$40M. Punchbowl is the exception ($10M year one) because sponsors, not readers, paid.
6. **Start in one geography, prove renewal, then expand.** CoStar: D.C. only for seven years. Curate: Wisconsin → six Midwest states → 50. Glenigan: UK only for 48 years. Cityscape: Chicago only, still.
7. **The moat is upstream access and accumulated history, defended by law.** Glenigan's three-week Planning Portal exclusive; Bloomberg's anchor-customer exclusivity; CoStar's photo copyrights and injunctions; Dodge's 130 years of normalized records. Nobody's moat was the scraper.
8. **Buyers are adjacent data companies**, not VCs: S&P (Panjiva), Morningstar (PitchBook), Verisk (BuildFax), Altus (Reonomy), Roper (ConstructConnect), FiscalNote (Curate), CoStar (Zonda), LexisNexis (CourtLink), Byggfakta (Glenigan).
9. **Edges die four ways:** the government publishes it well (EDGAR), the subjects opt out (manifests), the input commoditizes (satellite imagery), or the company over-expands past its renewing base (Xceligent, FiscalNote).

## What this means for a town-agenda feed in New England (315 towns, MA/NY/RI)

1. **Sell to the party that loses money by being late, and price it like Politico Pro.** Solar and battery developers, land-use lawyers, telecom site-acquisition firms, data-center site teams, and homebuilders each have someone paid to watch 315 agendas badly. Charge per organization per topic (solar, BESS, data centers, zoning/housing, towers) in the $3,000–5,000/year range Politico Pro ($3,295) and Quorum ($4,800) proved at launch, with a $300–500/year individual tier the way Punchbowl ($300) and Capitol Fax ($500) do. Analogue: Curate's contractors paid for a weekly spreadsheet of what boards were discussing before the RFP; that sold for eight figures in five years.
2. **Build the archive and the normalization, not just the alert.** Every agenda item tagged by town, parcel, topic, board, and outcome, searchable back to day one, so a buyer can ask "which towns moved on BESS bylaws this quarter and what passed." Chicago Cityscape's Lead Finder (proposed projects and zoning changes) is the one-person version; CoStar's accumulated, re-verified database is the $3B version. Data Center Watch turned the same kind of reading into a "$18B blocked, $46B delayed" tally that landed on NBC; a quarterly New England "what boards decided" count is the same move and the cheapest marketing available.
3. **Get upstream of the website.** Ask each of the 315 clerks to email agenda packets and Open Meeting Law postings the moment they're filed; cultivate the clerks, not the portals. Glenigan's 2025 Planning Portal deal gets applications three weeks before council websites; Dodge's edge in 1891 was a man with index cards who knew the permit clerks. Clerks who know you by name are the moat no competitor's scraper copies, and SmartProcure's FOIA friction shows what happens when you're a faceless request.
4. **Fund the first year with a sponsor or two anchor customers, not seats.** Punchbowl's $10M first year was ~90% sponsorship; Bloomberg's Merrill Lynch paid for the build before anyone else could buy; Quorum lived on competition prizes until renewals carried it. One regional solar developer or land-use law firm paying $10–20K for first look at all 315 towns, or a sponsor on the free daily, buys the runway to reach the renewal data that sets the price.
5. **Stay inside the lines and expect the government to catch up.** Read only what boards post; never republish another service's write-ups or photos (Xceligent's $500M judgment, CoStar v. CREXi); respect the terms of the agenda vendors' portals, because terms of service beat "it was public" in hiQ; don't build profiles of residents who speak at hearings (Clearview's €30.5M); don't trade or tip on anything a staffer tells you before it's posted (Blaszczak). And assume Massachusetts or a vendor will one day publish a decent statewide agenda search for free (EDGAR). Sell the synthesis, the history, and the phone call to the clerk, which stay valuable after the raw feed is free.
