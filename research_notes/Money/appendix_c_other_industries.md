# Where else does a public record precede a money event?

A survey for Agenda Ahead, written 26 October 2026.

We have a machine that reads about 315 town-government agendas every morning, pulls out the items on chosen subjects, writes one plain sentence per item, ranks them, and publishes pages, CSVs and alerts. This report asks where else the same machine could be pointed. For each candidate stream it says what the record is, where it lives, what money follows it and how long after, who pays for that information today and roughly what, who already sells it, and how much of our crawler and writing pipeline would carry over. It ends with a ranking and a first-month plan for the top three.

Two rules were kept throughout. Every number has a link; where no price could be found, that is said rather than guessed. And "well served" is said plainly where it is true.

## The test applied to each stream

1. Lead time: how long between the record and the money (a contract, an opening, a purchase, a loan)?
2. Who pays: is there a buyer already spending money on this signal, and what do they pay now?
3. Competition: is the stream already aggregated and sold, or is it scattered across thousands of sites and read by nobody?
4. Reuse: does it come from the same town websites, in the same PDF-agenda form, that our crawler and sentence-writer already handle?

The short version: the federal and state streams (FDA, FCC, USPTO, customs, UCC, court dockets, procurement, interconnection queues) are already aggregated and sold by many vendors, often cheaply. The scattered local streams (planning-board filings, Town Meeting warrants, liquor-license hearings, school-committee agendas, wetlands filings) are read by few, sold by fewer, and come from the same 315 town websites we already crawl.

## The candidates

### 1. Building permits and certificates of occupancy

- **The record:** a permit application or issuance (new construction, addition, roof, solar, HVAC, electrical), and later a certificate of occupancy.
- **Where it lives:** scattered across town and city permit portals (in Massachusetts many towns use a handful of vendor systems, each with its own pages), plus city open-data feeds in large cities. Nationally it is already aggregated: [BuildZoom](https://www.independent.com/2016/10/06/buildzoom-connects-you-contractors/) built its business on harvested permit and license records; [Shovels](https://www.shovels.ai/pricing) sells cleaned permit, contractor and property records through an API.
- **Money event and lag:** the permit precedes the work by weeks; a roofing or solar permit tells a materials supplier, a lender or an insurer that spending is about to happen. A CO tells furniture, appliance, landscaping and moving vendors that a household is about to arrive.
- **Who pays today and how much:** Shovels lists Free ($0, 500 credits/month), Basic ($599/month, 25,000 credits) and Pro ($999/month, 50,000 credits), with custom enterprise data licenses ([Shovels pricing](https://www.shovels.ai/pricing)). Sacra estimates Shovels at about $1.5M ARR at the end of 2025 and about $3M ARR by August 2026, founded 2022, $6.5M raised, customers "still numbering in the dozens" ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/shovels/)); those are Sacra's estimates, not company figures. Construction Monitor sells permit-based lead reports; one trade article quoted its Los Angeles subscription at $96 for one month, $540 for six months or $960 for a year ([JLC](https://www.jlconline.com/business/sales-marketing/pulled-permits-as-a-source-of-leads/); undated, so treat as old). BuildZoom charges contractors a 2.5% referral fee on jobs won ([Santa Barbara Independent](https://www.independent.com/2016/10/06/buildzoom-connects-you-contractors/)). Low-end scrapers on Apify sell permit rows from $1.60 per 1,000 ([Apify](https://apify.com/lowlanddata/building-permit-monitor)) and $4.00 per 1,000 ([Apify](https://apify.com/lavender_whistle/building-permits-monitor)).
- **Who sells it:** Shovels, BuildZoom, Construction Monitor, HBW, Dodge Construction Network (custom pricing: [Dodge](https://www.construction.com/what-is-dodge-construction-network-the-dodge-report-explained/); a third party describes its target as commercial GCs with $50M+ revenue: [Meltplan](https://www.meltplan.com/blogs/buildingconnected-alternatives-what-gc-precon-teams-are-actually-switching-to-in-2026)), ConstructConnect (a third party puts it at roughly $3,600/year and up: [Meltplan](https://www.meltplan.com/blogs/buildingconnected-alternatives-what-gc-precon-teams-are-actually-switching-to-in-2026)), PermitGrab ([SaaSHub](https://www.saashub.com/permitgrab)), many Apify actors.
- **How hard for us:** medium. Permit portals are not agendas; each vendor system needs its own scraper, and the data is tabular, so our sentence-writer adds little. The one place our crawler helps is upstream of the permit: the planning-board and ZBA approvals that precede large permits by months (see 3).
- **Verdict:** well served at every price point. Not a first move. Useful later as enrichment ("the project we flagged at planning board in March pulled its building permit in September").

### 2. Liquor licenses, common-victualler and health permits (restaurant openings)

- **The record:** an application for a new or transferred alcohol license, a common-victualler (food service) license, an entertainment license, or a food-establishment permit. In Massachusetts the local licensing authority (usually the select board) holds a public hearing under M.G.L. c. 138 before the state ABCC acts; the hearing is a posted agenda item naming the applicant, the premises and the license type. Examples: [Halifax](https://www.halifax-ma.org/node/50541), [Granby](https://www.granby-ma.gov/sites/g/files/vyhlif4466/f/events/04-22-24-bos.pdf), [Mendon](https://www.mendonma.gov/sites/g/files/vyhlif881/f/events/qt_restaurant_llc_dba_alicante_public_hearing_notice.pdf).
- **Where it lives:** the local hearing is scattered across town agendas, which is exactly what we crawl. The state-level record (ABCC approvals; TABC in Texas, ABC in California, SLA in New York) is aggregated per state and is what the existing vendors read. The ABCC reported issuing 24,965 licenses in calendar 2012 (figure as it appears in the report; the year attribution is not fully clear) ([ABCC 2013 annual report](https://www.mass.gov/doc/2013-alcoholic-beverages-control-commission-annual-report/download)) and processes renewals for roughly 12,000 retail pouring and package licenses ([state audit document](https://www.mass.gov/doc/alcoholic-beverages-control-commission/download)); no per-year count of new applications was found.
- **Money event and lag:** a new license application means a bar, restaurant or store opens in roughly 30 to 90 days ([Apify TX/CA listing](https://apify.com/rook-data-tools/new-liquor-license-leads)); another vendor says applications appear 60 to 90 days before doors open ([Apify multi-state listing](https://apify.com/malonestar/liquor-license-new-openings-tracker)). The local hearing comes before the state filing, so it is earlier still. Before opening, the operator buys kitchen equipment, a POS system, insurance, payroll, linens, signage, hood cleaning, and chooses beer, wine and spirits distributors.
- **Who pays today and how much:** Restaurant Activity Report (RAR) sells a twice-weekly pre-opening lead report to equipment and supply vendors ([Total Food Service](https://totalfood.com/rar-restaurant-activity-report-brings-vendor-community-easy-accurate-lead-source/)); the only price found is a 2019 promotion that valued a one-month subscription at $1,150 ([Starfleet Media](https://starfleetmedia.com/solutions/preopening-restaurants-resource/)). RAR's current site lists products but no prices ([RAR](https://www.restaurantactivityreport.com/)). The Restaurant Data Company says it produces about 2,000 new-opening leads a month from legal filings and sells Team, Team Plus and Enterprise plans, prices not published ([Dealroom](https://app.dealroom.co/companies/the_restaurant_data_company)). Apify actors sell state liquor-license rows from $8.25 per 1,000 ([Apify](https://apify.com/malonestar/liquor-license-new-openings-tracker)) and Chicago food-service license rows from $5.00 per 1,000 ([Apify](https://apify.com/rook-data-tools/new-food-service-license-leads)).
- **Who else is in it:** What Now Media Group runs local news sites in 33 cities that break restaurant and retail openings from permits and alcohol-license applications ([What Now Dallas launch](https://whatnow.com/dallas/retail/welcome-to-what-now-dallas-your-new-go-to-for-brick-and-mortar-breaking-news); [example story from an alcohol-license filing](https://whatnow.com/san-francisco/restaurants/new-dining-concept-maggie-macs-opening-in-inner-sunset/)). Its revenue model was not found in public sources.
- **How hard for us:** easy. These items are already in the agendas we read; the change is a new subject classifier and a sentence template that names applicant, premises, license type and hearing date.
- **Verdict:** a proven buyer set, moderate competition that reads the state record rather than the earlier local hearing, and near-total reuse. Top three.

### 3. Planning-board, ZBA and conservation-commission filings beyond energy (subdivisions, big-box retail, warehouses, data centers, cannabis, 40B housing)

- **The record:** site plan review, special permit, variance, definitive subdivision plan, wetlands Notice of Intent, host community agreement, and the continued hearings that follow. Each is an agenda item months before any permit or contract.
- **Where it lives:** scattered across the same town agendas and packets we already crawl. Nobody publishes a statewide list.
- **Money event and lag:** the approval precedes site work, construction contracts, equipment orders, leases and financing by six months to two years. For a warehouse or data center the dollar amounts are large; for a subdivision the buyers are the lumber yard, the paving contractor, the title company and the lender.
- **Who pays today and how much:** this is where the "meeting intelligence" companies sit. Curate (Madison, WI) read city and county agendas and minutes to find construction projects before an RFP, grew to more than 150 clients and all 50 states on a $1.65M seed-plus round ([BizTimes](https://biztimes.com/project-pitch-it-ai-startup-curate-expands-into-all-50-states/?amp=1); [UW–Madison](https://news.wisc.edu/uw-madison-spinoff-gives-builders-a-head-start-on-new-projects)) and was bought by FiscalNote in August 2021, terms undisclosed ([FiscalNote](https://fiscalnote.com/newsroom/fiscalnote-announces-acquisition-of-curate); [gener8tor](https://gener8tor.com/news/gener8tor-alumni-curate-solutions-bought-by-washington-dc-firm)). Civic IQ says it monitors 30,000+ public meetings a month across 80,000+ agencies ([Civic IQ Signals](https://civiciq.com/products/signals)), and 100,000+ agencies on its MCP page ([Civic IQ](https://civiciq.com/mcp/public-meetings-mcp)); it does not publish prices ([G2](https://www.g2.com/products/civic-iq/pricing)). Hamlet processes meeting video from more than 3,000 local governments ([PublicCEO](https://www.publicceo.com/2026/02/hamlet-launches-nationwide-public-meeting-coverage-over-3000-local-governments-videos-now-discoverable/)), sells a $49/month plan, has about 20 customers on its higher-end products, and has raised about $10M; its early paying customers were real-estate developers, a data-center developer, a PAC and retailers ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=3073247)). For the opposition side of the same filings, Heatmap Pro sells a database of contested clean-energy and data-center projects and local laws built from calls to all 3,100+ counties, price on request ([Heatmap Pro](https://heatmap.news/st/Landing_Pro_Subscription)); it reported at least $260 billion of data-center investment canceled this year after local opposition ([Heatmap](https://heatmap.news/politics/local-opposition-data-center-cancellations)). Data Center Watch (10a Labs) counted $64 billion of data-center projects blocked or delayed over two years ([report](https://datacenterwatch.org/report)) and $98 billion in March–June 2025 alone ([Data Center Knowledge](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/regulations/local-opposition-hinders-more-data-center-construction-projects)); critics note its "blocked" category is loose.
- **Adjacent incumbents by sub-market:** for subdivisions and lots, Zonda (Metrostudy) sells subscription data on future lot supply and projects; CoStar agreed to buy Zonda for $800 million in May 2026, completed August 2026 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zonda_Home)). For warehouses and big-box, CoStar is the incumbent; third-party estimates put it at $200–500 per user per month ([ThePricer](https://www.thepricer.org/how-much-does-costar-membership-cost)) or a median of about $40,000 a year ([Brainbound](https://brainbound.blog/costar-pricing-guide)); neither is a CoStar figure. Chain Store Guide sells retail-chain expansion databases at $2,500, $3,300 and $4,500 a year ([Chain Store Guide](https://www.chainstoreguide.com/category/leading-chain-tenant-leads)). For cannabis, Cannabiz Media tracks 300,000+ licenses with "solution based pricing" on request ([Cannabiz pricing](https://www.cannabiz.media/pricing)); an older alert product started at $50/month ([Cannabiz](https://www.cannabiz.media/marijuana-license-alerts-now-available-from-cannabiz-media)). In Massachusetts the Cannabis Control Commission will not consider a license until a host community agreement is signed ([Cannabis Business Times](https://www.cannabisbusinesstimes.com/article/massachusetts-host-community-agreements)), and the HCA is negotiated and voted at select board meetings ([Maynard process](https://townofmaynard-ma.gov/DocumentCenter/View/748)), so the local agenda is the earliest public record. Zoneomics sells zoning-code data (not filings) at $61 and $186 a month ([Zoneomics](https://www.zoneomics.com/pricing)).
- **Where the fights are:** warehouse proposals in New Jersey have drawn nine-hearing planning-board sagas and hardened statewide opposition ([Yahoo/MyCentralJersey](https://news.yahoo.com/hillsborough-planning-board-agenda-packed-085708995.html); [CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/156658731/warehouse-opposition-hardens-in-new-jersey-against-proposed-megaprojects)). No public tracker of warehouse applications was found.
- **How hard for us:** easy. Same sources, new subject list, sentence templates that carry applicant, parcel, square footage or unit count, and next hearing date.
- **Verdict:** the largest edge, the most buyers with money, and the most reuse. The national players (FiscalNote/Curate, Civic IQ, Hamlet) cover breadth with keyword search; none of them writes a plain sentence per item for a defined region and sells it to the local trades. Top three.

### 4. School-committee agendas (edtech and school construction)

- **The record:** school committee agendas and packets (curriculum adoptions, technology purchases, bus contracts, food service, HVAC and roof projects), plus the Massachusetts School Building Authority pipeline: a district files a Statement of Interest, is invited into a 270-day Eligibility Period, then a Feasibility Study, and only then procures a designer and owner's project manager through MSBA's RFS process ([MSBA prerequisites](https://www.massschoolbuildings.org/building/prerequisites)). In December 2025 MSBA staff recommended inviting 15 districts from 27 SOIs ([MSBA memo](https://www.massschoolbuildings.org/sites/default/files/edit-contentfiles/About_Us/Board_Meetings/2025_Board/12.12.2025/Core_Program_EP_Invitations_Memo.pdf)).
- **Where it lives:** scattered; many districts use BoardDocs or similar portals rather than the town site. MSBA board materials are aggregated on one site.
- **Money event and lag:** a curriculum or technology item on a committee agenda precedes a purchase by one to six months; the MSBA pipeline precedes a building contract by two to four years.
- **Who pays today and how much:** Burbio's School Board Meeting Tracker covers board documents for suppliers, with alerts and CRM feeds ([Burbio](https://about.burbio.com/school-board-meeting-tracker)); it reported covering just under 2,000 districts a month ([Burbio](https://about.burbio.com/weekly-updates/burbio-school-tracker-documenting-school-board-meetings)) and partnered with Domo in June 2025 ([BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250623245557/en/Domo-and-Burbio-to-Launch-AI-Powered-K-12-Education-Data-Intelligence-Tool)). No Burbio price was found. GovSpend sells purchase-order and bid data on annual contracts priced by conversation ([GovSpend FAQ](https://govspend.com/faq/)); a competitor's estimate from procurement data puts the median at about $11,576 a year, range $8,500 to $24,750 ([Fed-Spend](https://fed-spend.com/blog/govspend-pricing-2026-cost-alternatives)). EdWeek's Pro Access for districts starts at $4,200 a year ([EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/pro-access/pricing)); Market Brief enterprise membership is quote-based ([Market Brief](https://marketbrief.edweek.org/about/member-benefits/)). Civic IQ also covers school boards.
- **How hard for us:** medium. Where the school committee posts on the town site we already have it; where it uses BoardDocs we need one more scraper (BoardDocs is a single vendor, so one scraper covers many districts).
- **Verdict:** real buyers, but Burbio and Civic IQ are already there nationally. The defensible slice is regional: MSBA pipeline plus school-committee capital items for New England architects, OPMs and contractors. Rank 4.

### 5. Hospital and health-system filings (certificate of need; Massachusetts Determination of Need)

- **The record:** a CON or DoN application to add beds, operating rooms, imaging equipment or a new facility. Thirty-five states have CON programs ([Definitive Healthcare](https://definitivehc.com/resources/healthcare-insights/top-healthcare-organizations-certificates-need)).
- **Where it lives:** aggregated per state on an agency portal, e.g. [Massachusetts DPH pending DoN projects](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/don-pending-projects-and-applications) (six pending as of 22 December 2025) and [Connecticut's CON portal](https://portal.ct.gov/ohs/knowledge-base/articles/file-or-find-data/con/con-portal).
- **Money event and lag:** approval precedes construction and equipment purchase by one to three years; a single filing can be tens of millions of dollars.
- **Who pays today and how much:** Definitive Healthcare tracks more than 27,000 CONs from application to decision ([Definitive](https://definitivehc.com/resources/healthcare-insights/top-healthcare-organizations-certificates-need)); its contracts have a median of about $50,150 a year, range roughly $20,500 to $260,000 ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/definitive-healthcare)). Buyers are medical-equipment vendors, healthcare architects and builders, consultants and competing systems.
- **How hard for us:** easy to crawl (one page per state) but low volume and a different writing register (clinical and financial).
- **Verdict:** small, already served at the high end, and the filings are few. Rank 8. Worth a weekly line in a New England construction digest, not a product.

### 6. State regulatory dockets (DPU/PUC, EFSB, FERC, ISO-NE interconnection queue)

- **The record:** petitions, tariff filings, siting-board dockets, and queue entries for generation and storage projects.
- **Where it lives:** aggregated on one site per agency (e.g. [Pennsylvania PUC docket pages](https://www.puc.pa.gov/docket/M-2026-3061875)); ISO queues are public spreadsheets. Interconnection.fyi tracks 44,281 queue requests from 1995 to 2026 for free and is run by GridTracker, the firm behind LBNL's Queued Up data; developer names and full exports are sold through GridTracker ([interconnection.fyi](https://www.interconnection.fyi/)). Grid Status's open-source library returns the ISO-NE queue as a dataframe ([Grid Status docs](https://opensource.gridstatus.io/en/0.13.0/autoapi/gridstatus/index.html)). An Apify actor sells seven ISO queues from $4.40 per 1,000 rows ([Apify](https://apify.com/malonestar/interconnection-queue-tracker)).
- **Money event and lag:** a queue entry or siting docket precedes construction by two to six years; a rate case precedes utility capital spending by one to two years.
- **Who pays today and how much:** Stateside sells tiered utility-commission monitoring ([Stateside](https://stateside.com/utilities-monitoring)); Advanced Energy United's PowerSuite (DocketDash) is sold to non-members by subscription ([AEU](https://blog.advancedenergyunited.org/articles/aee-unveils-powersuite-a-new-online-platform-for-tracking-energy-legislation-and-regulatory-proceedings-in-all-50-states)); EnerKnol is a subscription SaaS for energy regulatory data ([Caplight](https://www.caplight.com/company/enerknol)). None publish prices. Enverus and S&P (Regulatory Research Associates) sell to the top of the market.
- **How hard for us:** medium. Few sites, but long legal PDFs, and the buyers already have tools.
- **Verdict:** not a new market; a natural add-on for our existing energy subscribers ("the town vote you saw in April is now docket EFSB 26-xx"). Rank 6.

### 7. Municipal procurement and bid notices

- **The record:** an RFP, IFB or RFQ.
- **Where it lives:** aggregated (COMMBUYS in Massachusetts, state portals elsewhere) and re-aggregated by many vendors.
- **Money event and lag:** the bid is the money event; there is no lead time left.
- **Who pays today and how much:** BidNet Direct supplier plans are $0, $599, $1,199 or $1,999 a year by number of states ([Civic IQ's price survey](https://civiciq.com/blog/civic-iq-bidnet-direct-pricing)); GovWin IQ starts at $1,000+ a month and BidPrime at $99–199 a month ([Gov Contract Finder](https://govcontractfinder.com/tools/government-contracting-software-pricing-guide)); Periscope S2G lists $59/month (state) and $119/month (national), billed annually ([Software Advice](https://www.softwareadvice.com/scm/bidsync-profile/)); GovSpend as above.
- **Verdict:** well served and commoditized. Rank 10, kept only because a bid notice closes the loop on a lead we flagged months earlier at planning board or Town Meeting, which is the stage none of these vendors sees.

### 8. UCC filings and tax liens

- **The record:** a UCC-1 financing statement (a lender took a security interest) or a federal/state tax lien.
- **Where it lives:** aggregated at each Secretary of State, often with paid bulk access.
- **Money event and lag:** a fresh UCC means a business just borrowed and may borrow again; a tax lien means a business needs tax resolution or expensive credit. Lag is days to months.
- **Who pays today and how much:** list brokers sell fresh UCC leads for $0.20 to $0.75 each and aged lists for $0.05 to $0.50, per one vendor ([Apify](https://apify.com/deadwood_data_solutions/ucc-filing-leads)); scrapers sell rows at $5 to $20 per 1,000 ([Apify](https://apify.com/intelscrape/ucc-lien-scraper)); a Florida federal-tax-lien daily feed sells from $3.00 per 1,000 ([Apify](https://apify.com/jobito/florida-lien-daily-feed)). Buyers are merchant-cash-advance brokers, equipment lenders and factoring companies.
- **Verdict:** commoditized, tabular, no writing needed, nothing of ours reuses. Below the line.

### 9. Court dockets and foreclosure notices

- **The record:** a new civil case, a probate filing, a foreclosure petition (in Massachusetts the Land Court petition is the first public step) and the later auction notice in a newspaper.
- **Where it lives:** aggregated (PACER, state court portals, registries); the notices are in newspaper legal notices (see 14).
- **Who pays today and how much:** PropertyRadar sells to investors at $119, $249 and $599 a month, or $99, $199 and $549 prepaid annually ([PropertyRadar](https://www.propertyradar.com/pricing)); UniCourt at $59, $199 and $399 a month ([UniCourt](https://unicourt.com/pricing)); Docket Alarm is listed by a third party at about $39.99 a month plus per-alert fees ([Spendbase](https://www.spendbase.co/?p=37198)). The Warren Group publishes Massachusetts Land Court foreclosure petitions weekly in Banker & Tradesman ([Warren Group](https://www.thewarrengroup.com/press-releases/foreclosure-starts-jump-45-6-percent-in-february-marking-the-24th-consecutive-month-of-increases/)); its prices were not found. Probate leads sell to real-estate agents at about $3.95 a lead in one promotion ([All The Leads podcast](https://realestate.buzzsprout.com/901660/episodes/16478346-probate-pre-probate-leads-q-a-all-the-leads-probate-mastermind-473)) and to lawyers at $65+ a lead ([CounselStack](https://www.counselstack.com/legal-leads/probate)).
- **Verdict:** heavily served, regulated, and far from our sources. Below the line.

### 10. FCC, FAA, FDA and USPTO filings

- **FDA:** 510(k) clearances are in openFDA, machine-readable; Apify actors sell change alerts from $4.00 per 1,000 rows ([Apify](https://apify.com/taroyamada/fda-510k-device-clearance-landscape-report)). The FY2027 510(k) review fee is $28,653 ([Pure Global](https://www.pureglobal.com/fr/videos/fda-510k-clearance-fy-2027-fee-estar)), so a clearance does precede spending on launch, but the buyers (distributors, consultants) already have Greenlight-type tools.
- **FAA:** the registry is a free bulk download; JETNET and AMSTAT sell enriched ownership and contact data to aircraft brokers ([Aviation Today](https://www.aviationtoday.com/2017/01/23/jetnet-offers-live-faa-tracking-data-with-aerodex-upgrade/)); an Apify actor sells owner rows at $10 per 1,000 ([Apify](https://apify.com/scrapemint/aircraft-owner-leads)). Prices for JETNET/AMSTAT were not found.
- **FCC:** equipment-authorization filings are the tech press's main source of unreleased hardware ([DroneXL](https://dronexl.co/es/us-government/fcc-filing/)); the money is in advertising, not subscriptions.
- **USPTO:** the clearest evidence that this feed has value is the fraud built on it: companies harvest applicant contacts from public trademark records and send misleading fee demands; the FTC and USPTO publish warnings and lists of such solicitations ([FTC](https://search.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2024/09/when-ip-stands-illegal-practices-protecting-your-business-trademark-deception); [USPTO](https://Www.Uspto.gov/sites/default/files/documents/tm-trademarks-scamprevention_20230427.pdf)). Legitimate trademark-watch services exist and are crowded.
- **Verdict:** all aggregated, all machine-readable, all served. Nothing of ours reuses. Below the line.

### 11. Customs records

- **The record:** ocean bills of lading for every US import.
- **Who pays today and how much:** ImportGenius lists Essentials at $125/month and Business at $399/month billed annually ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/10032153/ImportGenius/reviews/)); Panjiva (S&P) is reported around $10,000 a year by a competitor ([Kirchner](https://www.kirchnerdata.com/panjiva-free-alternative)); ImportYeti gives the search away free, sells Professional at $50/month billed annually and Enterprise from $1,000/month, and says it is cash-flow positive with over two million users and no venture money ([ImportYeti pricing](https://www.importyeti.com/pricing/supply-chain); [About](https://www.importyeti.com/about)). ImportYeti was built by one founder, David Applegate ([podcast](https://mywifequitherjob.com/episode453/)).
- **Verdict:** a good example of a one-person public-records business, and no fit for us. Below the line.

### 12. Job postings as expansion signals

- **The record:** a new posting on an employer's site.
- **Who pays today and how much:** Coresignal sells postings at roughly $0.005 to $0.196 each and Bloomberry at $0.003 to $0.006 ([Bloomberry](https://bloomberry.com/blog/the-best-job-posting-data-providers/)); LinkUp indexes employer sites nightly for quant buyers ([SignalBase](https://www.trysignalbase.com/best-job-posting-data-providers)); Revelio Labs sells derived workforce analytics on enterprise, sales-led pricing ([JobsPipe](https://jobspipe.dev/answers/how-much-does-revelio-labs-cost)).
- **Verdict:** commoditized and far from us. The one local use: a town posting "Director of Facilities" or a developer posting "Site Superintendent, [town]" confirms a project we flagged. Not a product. Below the line.

### 13. Notices of intent and environmental filings

- **Federal:** operators must file a Notice of Intent under the NPDES Construction General Permit before disturbing an acre or more; EPA's eNOI search shows NOIs under the 2017 and 2022 permits ([EPA](https://epa.gov/npdes/submitting-notice-intent-noi-notice-termination-not-or-low-erosivity-waiver-lew-under)). In Massachusetts EPA, not the state, runs the NPDES construction permit, so the federal eNOI list is a statewide list of construction starts with operator and site address. No vendor selling it as leads was found.
- **State:** MEPA publishes the Environmental Monitor twice a month, listing every new Environmental Notification Form by municipality ([mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/the-environmental-monitor); schedule set in [301 CMR 11.15](https://regulations.justia.com/states/massachusetts/301-cmr/title-301-cmr-11-00/section-11-15)). An ENF precedes construction by a year or more and names large projects only.
- **Local:** wetlands Notices of Intent go to the conservation commission, which we already crawl.
- **Verdict:** the NOI and the Monitor are aggregated and free, so they are a cheap enrichment for the development product (3), not a product of their own. The ConCom filings are already in our pipeline. Rank 5 as a feed, bundled into 3.

### 14. Local-newspaper legal notices

- **The record:** hearing notices, bid advertisements, foreclosure sale notices, probate citations, liquor hearings, name changes.
- **Where it lives:** Massachusetts law since 2015 requires notices published in newspapers to also appear on a statewide site run by the newspaper association ([Vineyard Gazette](https://vineyardgazette.com/node/83255)); that site, masspublicnotices.org, searches by keyword, county, town, publication and date, with 12 months in the current index ([masspublicnotices.org](https://masspublicnotices.org/)); the page mentions no RSS feed, email alert or API. Forty-seven state sites are linked from a national portal ([NNA Foundation](https://nnafoundation.org/newspaper-association-managers-group-launches-nationwide-public-notice-website)). Column, which raised $30 million in 2022 and charges a processing fee on each notice placed, announced a Public Notice API with 60+ structured notice types ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2022/08/09/exclusive-column-raises-30m-expanding-beyond-public-notice); [Column](https://www.column.us/blog/series-a/); [GovTech](https://govtech.com/biz/startup-public-notice-tech-firm-column-raises-30m)).
- **Money event and lag:** the notice is usually a copy of something we already see earlier on an agenda, except foreclosure sales and probate, which are sold by others (9).
- **Verdict:** a cross-check feed, not a product. One scraper of masspublicnotices.org would catch hearings in towns we do not crawl and confirm dates. Rank 7.

### 15. Town Meeting warrants as a whole

- **The record:** the warrant for an annual or special Town Meeting, posted on the town site in the days before the meeting, listing every article: operating budget, capital purchases, borrowing and debt exclusions, zoning bylaw changes, Community Preservation Act grants, land purchases, union contracts. Examples: [Dover](https://doverma.gov/273/Town-Meeting-Warrant); Hardwick voters approved borrowing $860,000 for a fire pumper ([Hardwick](https://www.hardwick-ma.gov/media/6571)); Ashfield borrowed $260,000 of an authorized $300,000 for a fire truck ([Greenfield Recorder](https://home.recorder.com/Fire-truck-debt-generator-purchase-among-decisions-at-Ashfield-Special-Town-Meeting-57380615)).
- **Where it lives:** scattered PDFs on roughly 300 town sites, which we already crawl. The articles are discussed months earlier at capital planning committee, finance committee and select board meetings, which we also crawl. No statewide warrant database or tracker was found in this survey.
- **Money event and lag:** the vote authorizes the money; the RFP or purchase follows in one to twelve months, and the bid aggregators (7) only see it then. The capital-plan discussion precedes the vote by six to eighteen months.
- **Who pays today and how much:** nobody sells this. The buyers exist: fire-apparatus and DPW-equipment dealers, municipal architects and OPMs, roofing, HVAC and energy-performance contractors, water and sewer engineers, bond underwriters and municipal financial advisors (debt exclusions), solar and EV vendors, land trusts and CPA consultants, and law firms and developers who watch zoning changes. What they pay for the nearest substitutes: BidNet $599–1,999 a year, GovSpend about $11,576 median (see 7 and 4).
- **How hard for us:** easy to medium. Same sites, same PDFs; the new work is an article parser (number, title, dollar amount, funding source, department, sponsor) and a results step from the minutes.
- **Verdict:** no competition found, high reuse, a time edge the bid services cannot match. The market is regional (New England), which caps size but also keeps the big players out. Top three.

### 16. Other streams noticed along the way

- **New business registrations (Secretary of State):** sold to insurance agents and merchant-services sellers; scrapers charge $10 per 1,000 rows and one listing says list services charge $50 to $200 a month for the same data ([Apify](https://apify.com/scrapemint/new-business-registration-leads)). West Virginia alone reported 1,181 new registrations in June 2025 ([WV SOS](https://sos.wv.gov/article/secretary-state-kris-warner-reports-1181-new-wv-business-registrations-june-2025)). Commoditized; no fit.
- **Board-agenda hosting is a separate, unrelated business:** BoardBook charges a district $3,225 for 2026–27 ([BoardBook](https://manuals.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/5b3f3fea-e0bf-4085-af7d-7c774e562f79?org=1122)); we read these, we do not need to host them.

## The top three, in detail

### 1. Development Ahead: the regional project pipeline

**First product.** A weekly page and CSV covering the towns we already crawl: every new site plan, special permit, variance, definitive subdivision, wetlands NOI, host community agreement and 40B filing, one sentence each (applicant, address or parcel, size in units or square feet, use, board, next hearing date, status), ranked by size and stage, with per-subject and per-town email alerts. Subjects to start: warehouses and distribution, data centers and battery storage, residential subdivisions and multifamily, retail over 20,000 square feet, cannabis, hotels, self-storage, car washes and drive-throughs. A free public digest of the ten biggest items each week for marketing.

**First ten customers, by type.** (1) Civil engineering and survey firms. (2) Land-use law firms. (3) Industrial and land brokers. (4) Site-work and general contractors. (5) Building-product and equipment reps (precast, steel, HVAC, elevators). (6) Community banks' commercial lenders. (7) Utilities' new-service planners and the gas and electric account managers. (8) Land trusts, watershed groups and opposition coalitions (cheaper tier). (9) Regional planning agencies and towns benchmarking neighbors. (10) Local newsrooms (cheapest tier).

**Price.** Anchors: Shovels $599–999/month for raw permit data; Hamlet $49/month for search; CoStar several hundred dollars per seat per month by third-party estimates; Chain Store Guide $2,500–4,500 a year. Proposed: $199/month per seat or $1,990 a year for a firm up to five seats; $49/month for nonprofits and newsrooms; the free weekly digest. The sentence-per-item is what the national keyword tools do not provide, and it is what a broker or engineer forwards to a client.

**First month.** Week 1: add the subject taxonomy and sentence templates; run them against the last 90 days of already-crawled agendas to produce a backfilled pipeline table. Week 2: add the MEPA Monitor and EPA eNOI as enrichment columns (ENF filed, NOI filed) and a masspublicnotices.org scraper for hearing cross-checks; publish the first weekly. Week 3: send the issue with a personal note to 40 named people across the ten types above, ask for 20 minute calls, offer three months at half price. Week 4: ship alerts and the CSV export; aim for five paying pilots and a list of the twenty projects they care about most, which becomes the ranking signal.

### 2. Warrant Watch: capital spending before the RFP

**First product.** A page and CSV of every article on every posted annual and special Town Meeting warrant in the crawled towns, parsed into: town, meeting date, article number, type (capital purchase, borrowing or debt exclusion, zoning or bylaw change, CPA grant, land acquisition, contract), department, dollar amount, funding source, and one plain sentence. Upstream, the same page lists capital-plan, finance-committee and select-board items that mention a future article, so a subscriber sees "pumper truck, $860,000, debt exclusion, Hardwick" in the January capital plan, not on the May warrant. Downstream, a results column filled from minutes and the state's bid portal, so the subscriber can see which authorizations have not yet gone out to bid.

**First ten customers, by type.** (1) Fire-apparatus dealers. (2) DPW and highway equipment dealers (plow trucks, loaders, sweepers). (3) Municipal architects and owner's project managers. (4) Roofing, HVAC and energy-performance contractors. (5) Water and wastewater engineering firms. (6) Municipal bond underwriters and financial advisors (debt exclusions and borrowing authorizations). (7) Solar, EV-charging and microgrid vendors. (8) Land trusts and CPA consultants (land and open-space articles). (9) Land-use law firms and developers (zoning bylaw articles). (10) Statewide associations and press.

**Price.** Anchors: BidNet $599–1,999 a year and GovSpend about $11,576 median a year for the later stage; nobody sells the earlier stage. Proposed: $149/month per seat or $1,490 a year; a season pass at $490 for January through June, when most annual warrants post; a vendor-category bundle (all fire and DPW capital articles in New England) at $2,990 a year. Keep the headline totals free; sell the article-level table and alerts.

**First month.** It is late October: fall special Town Meetings are posting now and spring warrants will post from March. Week 1: build the article parser on last spring's warrants, which we already have, and check it against the minutes for passed or failed. Week 2: tag capital-plan and finance-committee items in current agendas that name a future article; publish a backfilled "what towns authorized in 2026 and what has not gone to bid yet" page as the proof. Week 3: call fifteen equipment dealers and ten architects and engineers; the pitch is a named list of authorizations in their category with no RFP yet. Week 4: ship fall special-Town-Meeting alerts and open presales for the spring season.

### 3. Opening Ahead: restaurants before they open

**First product.** A weekly list, by town, of every hearing on a new or transferred alcohol license, common-victualler license, entertainment license, outdoor-seating approval, and any site plan or change-of-use for a restaurant, one sentence each (trade name, address, license type, applicant and proposed manager, hearing date, decision when known), plus a CSV with the licensee's mailing address as it appears in the notice. Dedupe against ABCC approvals so a subscriber sees the local hearing first and the state approval later as a status change.

**First ten customers, by type.** (1) Food-service equipment dealers. (2) POS and payments vendors. (3) Beer, wine and spirits distributors (a new licensee is a new account). (4) Commercial insurance brokers (liquor liability). (5) Payroll and HR vendors. (6) Linen and uniform services. (7) Hood-cleaning and fire-suppression contractors. (8) Sign shops and printers. (9) Restaurant brokers and retail leasing agents. (10) Local food media.

**Price.** Anchors: RAR's one month valued at $1,150 in 2019 for a national twice-weekly report; Apify rows at $5–8 per 1,000; probate leads at about $3.95 each. Proposed: $99/month per seat for one state, $249/month for New England, or $12 per lead with a monthly minimum for distributors who want only their counties. Cheaper than RAR because it is regional; earlier than RAR because it is the hearing, not the state filing.

**First month.** Week 1: classifier and templates for license items; backfill 90 days and reconcile against ABCC decisions to measure how many days of lead we actually have (publish that number; it is the sales pitch). Week 2: first weekly issue; build the mailing-address CSV. Week 3: email fifty equipment dealers, distributors and POS resellers with the issue attached and a count of openings in their territory; offer the first month free for a call. Week 4: alerts by county and license type; aim for five paying accounts and one distributor on the per-lead plan.

## What is well served, and where a small team can still win

Well served, do not enter head-on: building permits (Shovels, BuildZoom, Construction Monitor, Dodge, ConstructConnect, Apify at $1.60 per 1,000 rows); procurement (BidNet, GovWin, BidPrime, Periscope, GovSpend); UCC and liens; court dockets and foreclosures (PropertyRadar, UniCourt, Warren Group); federal filings (openFDA, FAA registry, FCC, USPTO) and the scrapers on top of them; customs (Panjiva, ImportGenius, ImportYeti); job postings (Coresignal, LinkUp, Revelio); CON at the enterprise level (Definitive at a ~$50K median contract).

Where a small team can still win: records that are scattered across hundreds of town websites as PDFs, that nobody aggregates, that precede the money by months, and that need a sentence rather than a row to be useful. That is planning-board and ZBA filings, Town Meeting warrants and the capital plans behind them, liquor-license hearings, school-committee capital items, and wetlands filings. The national meeting-intelligence firms (FiscalNote/Curate, Civic IQ, Hamlet, Burbio) prove the buyers exist and sell breadth by keyword; a regional product that writes the item in plain words for a named trade is a different thing, and it is the thing we already make.
