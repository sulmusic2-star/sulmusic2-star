# What Agenda Ahead can predict, how early, and who would pay

*Research memo for the owner, 26 October 2026. Web sources are linked inline. Internal figures come from `reports/Meeting monitor deep dive.md` and `scratchpad/research/ledger_notes.md` and are marked "internal". Where I could not find a number, I say so rather than estimate one.*

## The short version

Agenda Ahead reads about 315 town agendas a day in Massachusetts, New York and Rhode Island and flags siting items before the meeting. That stream predicts six kinds of money event. Ranked by (value to the buyer) × (how early we see it) × (how reliably our data shows it), the first five are worth building as paid products; the sixth and below are not, or not yet.

| Rank | What the agenda item predicts | Lead over the money event | Who pays | Score (V×E×R, each 1–5) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Whether a Massachusetts solar or battery bylaw voted at Town Meeting will survive Attorney General review, and which clauses will not | Weeks before the vote; the AG ruling comes 90–180 days after it | Solar/BESS developers, their lawyers, land brokers | 3×5×5 = 75 |
| 2 | New York battery-storage moratoria: adoption, extension and expiry, town by town | 1–3 months before adoption; months before expiry | Storage developers, their counsel, lenders | 5×3×4 = 60 |
| 3 | Housing that will be permitted and built (40B comprehensive permits, MBTA-3A district projects) | 6–24 months before groundbreaking | Contractors, suppliers, construction-lead vendors, lenders | 3×4×5 = 60 |
| 4 | Data-center moratoria and bans in the three states (the ledger) | Weeks to months before the vote | Developers, land investors, utilities, PR and law firms, national opposition-data vendors | 4×3×4 = 48 |
| 5 | A restrictive zoning change is coming, and the filing window before it closes | Weeks to months before "first notice" in MA; before adoption in NY | Housing and land developers, land-use attorneys, title/zoning report firms | 4×4×3 = 48 |
| 6 | Where opposition is organising (for engagement and PR) | Months | Community-relations and public-affairs firms, lobbyists | 3×4×3 = 36 |
| 7 | Which towns will actually host large loads (an input to utility and ISO load forecasts) | Years | Utilities, ISOs, state regulators | 3×4×3 = 36 |
| 8 | Wetlands permits and cell-tower permits as construction leads | 1–6 months | Contractors, tower-lease buyers | 2×3×4 = 24 |
| 9 | Anything a hedge fund would trade | n/a | REIT and utility traders | 1×2×2 = 4 |

Local newsrooms score well on timing and reliability but pay little; they are the free tier and the distribution channel, not a paying segment (see §6).

Two facts shape everything below. First, the state level is moving faster than the towns: New York's Executive Order 62 (14 July 2026) pauses discretionary state permits for data centers of 50 MW or more for up to a year ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/07/new-york-enacts-first-statewide-data-center-moratorium)), Massachusetts' 2024 climate law gives every clean-energy project under 25 MW / 100 MWh a single local permit that must be decided in 12 months and larger ones a single state permit in 15 months ([Barclay Damon](https://www.barclaydamon.com/alerts/massachusetts-passes-climate-bill-accelerating-siting-and-permitting-for-clean-energy-projects)), and the Massachusetts AG has struck every solar/BESS moratorium she has ruled on since the SJC's *Tracer Lane II* decision of June 2022 (internal: 18 of 18 in 14 towns). Second, the thing our data is weakest on is outcomes: we read agendas, not minutes, so until outcome tracking exists every "prediction" must be sold as a base rate with counts and links, not as a forecast about a named town.

## 1. The clocks that turn an agenda line into money

Each state fixes the minimum time between a proposal appearing and a rule taking effect. These are the lead times a buyer can bank on; our alert adds the days between the agenda posting and the meeting on top.

**Massachusetts zoning (G.L. c. 40A §5).** The Select Board must refer a proposed zoning amendment to the Planning Board within 14 days; the Planning Board must hold its hearing within 65 days; newspaper notice runs in two successive weeks with the first publication at least 14 days before the hearing; if Town Meeting has not voted within six months of that hearing a new hearing is required; most amendments need a two-thirds vote, but as-of-right multifamily, mixed-use, ADU and several other housing amendments pass by simple majority; a defeated proposal cannot return for two years unless the Planning Board recommends it ([c. 40A §5](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleVII/Chapter40A/Section5)). The Town Clerk then has 30 days to send the bylaw to the Attorney General, who has 90 days to approve or disapprove, extendable by up to 90 more ([Municipal Law Unit](https://www.mass.gov/the-municipal-law-unit); [Sherborn 2025 extension letter](https://sherbornma.org/DocumentCenter/View/3605/Attorney-General-60-Day-Extension-ATM-2025-Articles)). Adding those clocks together (and noting that cities do not go to the AG), the shortest path from "first agenda mention" to "effective bylaw" works out to about 2–3 months in a city and 4–9 months in a town; Lowell's two months from first vote to adoption fits. The warrant article is visible on an agenda before any of it.

**The Massachusetts filing window (c. 40A §6).** A building permit or special permit issued before "the first publication of notice of the public hearing" on a zoning change is protected from it; a definitive subdivision plan filed before the change takes effect freezes zoning for eight years; an ANR plan freezes it for three; a special permit holder has three years to commence work ([c. 40A §6](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleVII/Chapter40A/Section6)). This is why "the Planning Board will discuss a draft bylaw" is worth money in Massachusetts: the race is to file before the first notice is published.

**New York.** A town must publish notice at least ten days before the hearing on a zoning change and give written notice to any adjacent municipality within 500 feet ([Town Law §264](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/TWN/264)); where General Municipal Law §239-m applies, the board cannot act until the county planning agency responds or 30 days pass ([Dutchess County town code example](https://ecode360.com/5090261)). The protection runs the other way from Massachusetts: under *Rocky Point Drive-In v. Brookhaven*, an amendment adopted while an application is pending applies to that application; rights vest only with a valid permit plus substantial construction and expenditure, and the "special facts" exception requires proof of bad-faith delay and rarely succeeds ([Jaspan](https://jaspanllp.com/news/rocky-point-drive-in-l-p-v-town-of-brookhaven-the-special-facts-rule); [*Westbury Laundromat*](https://www.nycourts.gov/REPORTER/3dseries/2009/2009_04072.htm)). In New York the agenda signal therefore predicts a loss, not a window: a developer who sees a moratorium coming has weeks to decide whether to keep spending.

**Rhode Island.** Notice of a council hearing on a zoning amendment must be published once a week for three successive weeks, posted and placed on the town website at least 14 days before, and mailed to any municipality within 200 feet ([R.I.G.L. §45-24-53](https://webserver.rilegislature.gov/Statutes/TITLE45/45-24/45-24-53.htm)); the planning board must report to the council within 45 days of receiving the proposal ([Coventry staff report citing §45-24-51](https://coventryri.gov/sites/coventryri.gov/files/attachments/planning/1119%20Tiogue%20Ave%20Zone%20Change%20-%20Staff%20Report%20-%2008-20-2026.pdf)).

**Clean-energy permits.** Massachusetts: 12 months for a consolidated local permit (under 25 MW or 100 MWh), 15 months for the EFSB (larger), automatic approval if the deadline is missed, DOER regulations effective 27 February 2026, and an 1 October 2026 deadline for towns to accept consolidated applications ([Barclay Damon](https://www.barclaydamon.com/alerts/massachusetts-passes-climate-bill-accelerating-siting-and-permitting-for-clean-energy-projects); [Foley Hoag](https://www.foleyhoag.com/news-and-insights/blogs/energy-and-climate-counsel/2026/march/massachusetts-ago-rejects-yet-another-municipal-solar-and-battery-storage-moratorium/)). New York: ORES issues a single permit preempting local permits for renewables of 25 MW or more ([DPS](https://dps.ny.gov/rapid-act-proposed-regulations-overview)), but a bill-stage analysis says the RAPID Act did not extend ORES to standalone storage, which stays with towns ([Hodgson Russ](https://sitepilot09.firmseek.com/client/hodgson/www/printpilot-publication-14449.pdf)); bills to change that were still in committee in May 2026 ([E+E Leader](https://www.environmentenergyleader.com/stories/new-york-battery-storage-moratoriums-hit-98-municipalities,126655)).

**Housing (40B).** The ZBA must open its hearing within 30 days of filing and decide within 40 days of closing it ([Middleton hearing schedule](https://middletonma.gov/DocumentCenter/View/3886/ZBA-Villebridge-Hearing-Schedule)); appeals to the Housing Appeals Committee take years (Manchester-by-the-Sea: appeal September 2022, evidentiary hearings March 2024, decision expected late 2025, per the [town](https://manchester.ma.us/729/40B) and the [Cricket](https://www.thecricket.com/stories/chapter-40b-appeal-is-a-slow-process,4701)). Brookline's Hammond Street project went from project-eligibility letter (4 October 2023) to draft decision (April 2025), about 18 months ([Brookline](https://www.brooklinema.gov/1311/Chapter-40B-Status)). After the permit, the Census Bureau measures the gap from authorization to start at about a month for single-family homes ([Census](https://www.census.gov/construction/nrc/data/time.html); [NAHB](https://eyeonhousing.org/2023/06/home-building-takes-longer-in-2022/)).

**Wetlands.** A Conservation Commission hearing within 21 days of a Notice of Intent, an Order of Conditions within 21 days of closing the hearing, and under *Oyster Creek* (SJC 2007) a commission that misses the decision deadline loses jurisdiction to MassDEP ([GRSM](https://www.grsm.com/insight/massachusetts-wetland-protection-act-timelines/); [Franklin](https://franklinma.gov/639/Notice-of-Intent-NOI)).

**Cell towers.** FCC shot clocks of 90 days for collocations and 150 days for new towers, 60 days for Section 6409(a) eligible facilities requests ([Fierce](https://www.fierce-network.com/tech/fcc-institutes-shot-clock-for-tower-siting-applications); [FCC DA 19-277](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-19-277A1_Rcd.pdf)).

## 2. Subject by subject: the chain, the lead time, the buyer, the evidence

### 2.1 Data centers

**Chain.** Resident complaint or a developer's informal approach → "discussion of data centers" on a Select Board or Planning Board agenda → draft moratorium or bylaw → Planning Board hearing → Town Meeting or council vote → (MA towns) AG review → developer withdraws, pauses or refiles elsewhere → land options lapse or move, interconnection requests are withdrawn.

**Observed timelines.**
- Lowell, MA: council first backed a pause in January 2026, voted a one-year moratorium (plus a possible 180-day extension) on 10 March 2026; no new project triggered it ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/boston/data-center-development/massachusetts-city-imposes-one-year-moratorium-on-data-centers-133606)).
- Mansfield, MA: Annual Town Meeting on 5 May 2026 passed Article 23 allowing only Tier I (up to 2 MW) data centers by special permit and barring everything larger, with "no current proposals" on the table ([Boston.com](https://www.boston.com/news/local-news/2026/05/13/mansfield-becomes-first-town-in-mass-to-pass-near-total-ban-on-data-centers/)). Our archive holds the warrant item (the internal ledger lists it as Article 24 dated 14 April 2026, while Boston.com reports Article 23 passed on 5 May; reconcile before publishing).
- Leominster, MA: council vote 8-0 on 28 September 2026 for moratoria on data centers and BESS until 31 July 2027 (internal ledger).
- Also in Massachusetts in 2026: Westfield and Holyoke, Agawam's 270-day moratorium at first reading, Ayer's draft moratorium to 26 October 2027, Heath's 11 March 2026 hearing on a combined BESS/data-center moratorium, and a Boston council proposal to make data centers a prohibited use everywhere ([WAMC](https://www.wamc.org/news/2026-07-13/pioneer-valley-communities-rally-against-data-centers); [Reminder](https://thereminder.com/local-news/agawam-moves-toward-270-day-moratorium-on-data-centers/); [Ayer](https://www.ayer.ma.us/DocumentCenter/View/14737/Ayer-Zoning-Bylaws---10-26-2026---Data-Center-Moratorium); [Heath](https://townofheath.org/files/March_11_2026_Public_Hearing__BESS_Data_Center.pdf); [Boston.com](https://www.boston.com/news/local-news/2026/09/15/boston-city-council-to-consider-zoning-changes-that-would-bar-data-center-construction/)).
- East Fishkill, NY: hearing and unanimous vote the same night, 25 June 2026, a freeze on data centers over 20 MW until 1 July 2029, aimed at a Treetop Development campus that was never formally filed and had dropped out of the NYISO queue ([WAMC](https://www.wamc.org/2026-06-26/east-fishkill-passes-3-year-data-center-freeze); [DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/three-year-data-center-moratorium-passed-in-town-of-east-fishkill-new-york-state-blocking-planned-campus/)).
- Tonawanda, NY: residents objected "for months" to a 300 MW proposal; the developer paused its application on 3 June 2026 citing a NYISO backlog; the board voted a one-year moratorium on facilities of 20 MW or more on 3 August 2026 ([BTPM](https://www.btpm.org/local/2026-08-04/tonawanda-town-board-passes-data-center-moratorium)).
- Lysander, NY: 350 residents at the 7 May 2026 meeting; six-month moratorium adopted that night against a proposed 300 MW, 120-acre campus ([Rockefeller Institute](https://rockinst.org/blog/updates-on-the-cloud-more-moratoriums-on-data-centers/)).
- Counter-examples: Lansing, NY withdrew a proposed moratorium that would have halted a TeraWulf project ([Ithaca.com](https://www.ithaca.com/news/lansing/lansing-drops-moratorium-plans-that-would-have-halted-data-center-project/article_f69baf6c-b3bd-4fa9-8ca7-62a1dfc32584.html)); Massena, NY voted a moratorium down ([WCAX](https://www.wcax.com/2026/09/17/massena-town-board-votes-down-data-center-moratorium/)).

The pattern: once a moratorium is on an agenda it usually passes, often at the same meeting as the hearing. The usable lead is therefore the gap between the first "discussion" item and the vote (Lowell: two months; Carmel's battery case below: two months), plus our posting lead. The vote itself is rarely a surprise by the time it is scheduled.

**Money at stake.** Data Center Watch (10a Labs) counted at least 48 projects worth $156 billion blocked or stalled in 2025, cancellations rising from six in 2024 to 25 in 2025, more than 20 more killed in Q1 2026, and 188 opposition groups in 40 states ([Fortune via Yahoo, 18 May 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/sectors/technology/articles/communities-blocking-billions-data-centers-164903052.html)). Heatmap Pro puts 2026 cancellations after local opposition at about $260 billion and says about half of projects that met backlash were cancelled ([Heatmap](https://heatmap.news/daily/260-billion-data-center-opposition), paywalled). Treat both with care: Exponent notes the DCW report "does not directly attribute all data center project cancellations or suspensions to these groups", that some listed cancellations were a company that "submits a new proposal with similar characteristics in a different but nearby location", and that 10a Labs works for AI companies ([Exponent](https://www.exponent.com/article/report-claims-64bn-data-center-projects-blocked-or-delayed)). That "nearby location" remark is the only direct evidence I found for the rerouting step in the chain; the academic evidence is for the opposite spillover, that neighbours copy restrictions (see §2.3).

**Where the land money moves.** Altus Group's transaction sample puts Northern Virginia land at a median of about $2.8M per acre, Central Ohio, Phoenix and Atlanta at $100K–$460K, markets without committed power near $28K, and sites within half a mile of a high-voltage substation at roughly twice the price of sites two to five miles away ([Altus](https://www.altusgroup.com/insights/where-value-is-created-in-data-center-land/)). Avison Young counted about $6 billion of US land bought for future data centers in the first half of 2026, up 79% ([DCK](https://datacenterknowledge.com/data-center-construction/ai-data-center-land-buying-hits-6b-as-power-premiums-rise)). Acres' land-values note states the buyer's logic plainly: "The teams that read zoning trajectory early can acquire land at pre-trajectory pricing. The teams that wait for formal rezoning pay the post-trajectory premium" ([Acres](https://landvalues.acres.com/zoning-trajectory-data-center-land-costs)).

**The three-state reality check.** New York has a real pipeline: 48 large-load projects totalling more than 11 GW in the NYISO queue in January 2026, nearly 12 GW by May with two-thirds of requests filed in 2025, and a PSC proceeding (Case 26-E-0045) on who pays for upgrades ([Governor's office](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-announces-psc-proceeding-her-plan-ensure-data-centers-pay-their-fair-share); [DCK](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/regulations/new-york-confronts-the-data-center-boom-balancing-growth-and-grid-reform)). New England does not: ISO-NE's first large-load forecast found only two projects in formal study, at most 285 MW combined, with no effect on system demand before winter 2027/28 ([ISO Newswire](https://isonewswire.com/2026/05/18/iso-ne-establishes-forecast-framework-for-data-centers-other-large-loads/); [New Project Media](https://newprojectmedia.com/?p=19353)). Massachusetts towns are legislating ahead of proposals, which makes our ledger (63 rows across 59 municipalities: 50 MA, 7 RI, 2 NY; 177 data-center agenda hits across 70 towns in the current window, internal) a map of where a developer cannot go rather than a predictor of where one is about to be blocked. And EO 62 means that for anything of 50 MW or more in New York, the state, not the town, is the binding constraint for the next year ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/07/new-york-enacts-first-statewide-data-center-moratorium)). Town moratoria still bind the 20–50 MW tier (East Fishkill and Tonawanda both set 20 MW thresholds) and everything after the EO lapses.

**Who gathers this today.** Acres Intelligence "integrates civil monitoring data from Hamlet" so a developer can cross-reference parcels "with Hamlet's local government sentiment data", because moratoriums and plan changes "emerge from city council transcripts, planning commission debates, and township meetings" ([Acres, 20 July 2026](https://www.acres.com/blog/prevent-data-center-permitting-delays-leveraging-community-sentiment-political-risk-data)). Transect launched Data Center Pulse in April 2026 with moratorium and ordinance tracking and a county/sub-county sentiment heatmap, priced through a customer-success manager ([Business Wire](https://markets.financialcontent.com/startribune/article/bizwire-2026-4-21-transect-launches-data-center-pulse-product-to-identify-community-opposition-risks); [Transect support](https://support.transect.com/pulse)). Heatmap Pro sells an Opposition Risk Score for every county and ZIP code and a local-laws database it builds by calling each of 3,100+ counties, to development teams, external-affairs teams and "Project Finance & M&A" ([Heatmap Pro](https://heatmap.news/pro)). None of them publishes a price. Milldam PR launched a data-center community-relations practice in 2024 to "reduce risk, and accelerate project approvals" ([Data Center Frontier](https://www.datacenterfrontier.com/podcast/article/33038267/dcf-show-data-center-pr-practice-fosters-coalitions-community-outreach-to-reduce-development-backlash)); I found no fee data for such firms. Prince William County, VA shows the scale of political money around one campus: at least $198,070 of the board's $496,964 in 2020–22 donations came from people tied to the Digital Gateway ([InsideNoVA](https://www.insidenova.com/headlines/developers-focus-largesse-on-prince-william-county-democrats/article_93ebdf40-a30f-11ed-bcc7-7bd1d45fa8b7.html)).

**Buyers and value.** Developers and their lawyers (high value per project, but few projects in MA/RI); land investors (want the "pre-trajectory" signal in NY); utilities and the ISO (see §2.7); PR and community-relations firms; and the national vendors above, who phone counties and would license town-grain agenda data. Reliability of our signal: high. Moratorium items are explicit on agendas ("Data Center Moratorium", "Public Hearing: BESS/Data Center"). Lead: weeks to months before the vote.

### 2.2 Battery storage

**Chain.** Developer applies or approaches the town → fire-safety complaint → moratorium on the agenda → six- or twelve-month moratorium → extension hearings → either a regulating local law (tiers, setbacks) or a primary-use ban → the developer withdraws, loses non-refundable land and interconnection deposits, and the interconnection position is freed.

**Carmel, NY, start to finish.** Officials floated a moratorium in May 2024; a 19 June hearing drew hundreds; the board voted a six-month moratorium on 10 July 2024 against East Point Energy's 116 MW Union Energy Center on 95 acres ([News 12](https://westchester.news12.com/new-push-to-pause-plans-for-battery-storage-facility-in-carmel); [Examiner](https://theexaminernews.com/carmel-approves-six-month-moratorium-to-put-brakes-on-battery-farm)); after an October hearing the board banned systems over 600 kWh town-wide ([Westfair, 30 October 2024](https://westfaironline.com/combined/carmel-bans-large-scale-commercial-battery-energy-storage-systems)); East Point, owned by Equinor, withdrew a project it had been developing since early 2020 ([Energy-Storage.news](https://energy-storage.news/east-point-withdraws-116mw-bess-project-in-upstate-new-york-after-town-bans-utility-scale-storage)). Two months from first agenda signal to moratorium; five months to ban; four years of development spend lost.

**Scale in New York.** Carina Energy's monitor counted 98 active municipal BESS restrictions across 37 New York counties in spring 2026, 65% of the roughly 150 tracked nationally in 17 states; NY-BEST told legislators in January that more than 100 municipalities had moratoria stalling over 1 GW; many moratoria were expected to expire in the second half of 2026, especially in Chautauqua and Erie counties; and in Northampton, NY "opposition moved faster than the permitting process" for a 10 MW project ([E+E Leader, 19 May 2026](https://www.environmentenergyleader.com/stories/new-york-battery-storage-moratoriums-hit-98-municipalities,126655)). Modo Energy, using American Clean Power's count, put it at 108 authorities with moratoria or primary-use bans as of 23 November 2025, 50 with both, covering about 4,500 square miles or 8% of the state, and estimated from four hypothetical queue projects that a forced withdrawal forfeits $200,000–$800,000 for a small project and $500,000–$2.2 million for a large one in non-refundable land and interconnection costs ([Modo](https://modoenergy.com/research/en/nyiso-new-york-battery-permitting-landscape-moratoria-ban-risk)). Towns extend as readily as they adopt: Cortlandt has had a moratorium since January 2024 and held a 21 July 2026 hearing on extending it through 2026 ([Cortlandt notice](https://www.townofcortlandtny.gov/documents/PH-%20Battery%20Storage%20Moratorium%20for%207-21-2026.pdf)); Clifton Park adopted an extension on 21 July 2025 ([Clifton Park](https://cliftonparkny.gov/document-center/town-clerk-documents/town-clerk-bulletin-board-documents/5523-notice-of-adoption-battery-storage-extension/file)).

**Massachusetts is different.** The AG disapproved Blandford's solar/BESS moratorium on 11 March 2026 under c. 40A §3 and *Tracer Lane II*, as she had Northfield, Ware, Carver, Wareham, Leyden, Pelham, Spencer and Wendell ([Foley Hoag](https://www.foleyhoag.com/news-and-insights/blogs/energy-and-climate-counsel/2026/march/massachusetts-ago-rejects-yet-another-municipal-solar-and-battery-storage-moratorium/)); towns that want a pause now go to Beacon Hill for special acts (Worthington's H.5294, Becket's H.5641) ([Gazette](https://www.gazettenet.com/2026/04/09/worthington-residents-lobby-on-beacon-hill-for-temporary-solar-moratorium-in-their-town/); [H.5641](https://malegislature.gov/Bills/194/H5641.Html)). The project killer in Massachusetts is more often the grid: Hillman Energy withdrew its Tewksbury BESS after EFSB approval because "the company's cost to upgrade the grid greatly exceeded previous estimates" ([Boston 25](https://www.boston25news.com/news/local/surging-grid-upgrade-costs-force-cancellation-proposed-tewksbury-battery-storage-facility/4CZOAYKVQJBGXI6LH4V4ZTQXB4/)).

**Buyers and value.** Standalone-storage developers in New York, where the town is the permitting authority and the downside per project is six to seven figures; their counsel; project-finance lenders (Modo's cost figures are exactly what a lender's diligence asks). Reliability: high (items are explicit, including extension hearings). Lead: 1–3 months before adoption; the expiry calendar is known months out. Coverage is the constraint: our ledger has two New York municipalities; the moratoria are in 98–108.

### 2.3 Solar and clean-energy permit rules

**Chain.** Model bylaw or complaint → Planning Board drafts a solar/BESS bylaw → hearing → Town Meeting → AG review (MA) → regulating bylaw stands or moratorium falls → projects proceed, are redesigned, or are litigated for years → interconnection group study → commercial operation.

**Evidence that the AG step is predictable.** Internal record through 10 October 2026: the AG has disapproved all 18 solar or BESS moratorium articles decided since *Tracer Lane II* (14 towns), ten of them in 2026, while approving 109 of 114 regulating bylaws in whole or in part, usually striking specific clauses. Foley Hoag reads the same record as "almost certain to be rejected" ([Foley Hoag](https://www.foleyhoag.com/news-and-insights/blogs/energy-and-climate-counsel/2026/march/massachusetts-ago-rejects-yet-another-municipal-solar-and-battery-storage-moratorium/); [Mondaq on *Tracer Lane II*](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/renewables/1793056/solar-zoning-after-tracer-lane-ii-old-bylaws-new-risks)).

**Evidence that the fight is long and costly.** Sunpin applied for a Petersham special permit in April 2021, was denied on a 2-1 vote, lost in Land Court in 2023, won in the Appeals Court in 2025 and at the SJC on 14 July 2026, which sent the application back to the town: five years, no panels ([CommonWealth Beacon](https://commonwealthbeacon.org/courts/sjc-sides-with-solar-developer-in-dispute-with-petersham-officials/); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/sunpin-v-petersham-mass-sjc-narrows-path-denying-large-scale-solar)). PureSky's five Shutesbury projects (45 MW, planned in 2021, sued over bylaws in 2023) were paused in 2025 after the suit was dismissed without prejudice ([Amherst Bulletin](https://amherstbulletin.com/2025/09/07/puresky-pauses-shutesbury-solar-projects-after-court-ruling/)). Nationally, the Sabin Center's 2026 edition counts 865 local and 23 state restrictions, 70 added in 2025 (down from 112 in 2024 and 165 in 2023), 567 contested projects of which 227 (about 40%) were cancelled, 22 cancellations in 2025 totalling 9.8 GW, and New York leading the country with 52 contested projects ([pv magazine on Sabin 2026](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/09/08/the-u-s-saw-a-14-percent-increase-in-contested-renewable-projects-9-8-gw-in-cancellations-in-2025/); [Sabin 2025 edition](https://climate.law.columbia.edu/content/opposition-renewable-energy-facilities-united-states-june-2025-edition)). I could not retrieve the state-level appendix for Massachusetts or Rhode Island.

**Evidence that restrictions spread to neighbours.** Ko, Dolšak and Prakash found 729 of 3,143 US counties with restrictive renewable ordinances and a contagion effect from adjacent counties ([PLoS ONE 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10718419/)); Lerner found counties more likely to adopt wind ordinances when neighbours had ([Review of Policy Research 2022](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/revpol/v39y2022i2p120-142.html)); NREL modelled that existing setbacks could cut wind and solar resource by up to 87% and 38% ([Nature Energy 2023](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/natene/v8y2023i9d10.1038_s41560-023-01319-3.html)). This supports a "next town over" alert for developers with sites in neighbouring towns, which the site's neighbour rule already produces.

**The interconnection tail.** Massachusetts group studies covered about 679 MW of solar, some projects that applied three years earlier facing roughly five more years for upgrades ([pv magazine](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/?p=7548)); DPU opened docket 25-48 on 27 May 2025 ([Nuwatt summary](https://nuwattenergy.com/en/massachusetts/interconnection-reform-dpu-25-48-ma)); one project tied to a 2029 upgrade may miss the 31 December 2027 tax-credit deadline ([MarketScale](https://www.marketscale.com/industries/energy/massachusetts-queue-delays-put-2027-solar-tax-credits-at-risk-1196721)). Nationally, Berkeley Lab reports 2,061 GW active at end-2025, more than 750 GW withdrawn in 2025, only 13% of 2000–2020 capacity online and 75% withdrawn, a median of over three years to an interconnection agreement and over five to operation ([APPA on Queued Up, 1 July 2026](https://www.publicpower.org/node/30574); [LBNL](https://emp.lbl.gov/queued-2026-edition-characteristics-power-plants-seeking-transmission-interconnection-end-2025)). The lesson for pricing: a town bylaw signal is one of several gates, and the grid gate is slower than ours.

**The landowner money.** Warren County, NY approved 25-year leases to Nexamp at $3,765 and $4,607 per acre per year with 2% escalators ([Warren County resolution](https://public.warrencountyny.gov/home/Dynamicfolders/BOS/2021/Board%20Meetings-Resolutions/04.16.2021/R114%202021.pdf)); a land-services firm's marketing range is $10–$40 per acre per year during a 1–5 year option and $400–$2,000 per acre per year over 25–30 years ([LandGate](https://landgate.com/news/hidden-value-of-solar-rights)). A bylaw that bars ground-mount solar on a parcel ends that income stream; landowners and the brokers who place options are buyers of the signal, but small ones.

**Buyers and value.** Solar/BESS developers (internal estimate of 120–250 Massachusetts projects a year), their lawyers and permitting consultants, land brokers, DOER/MassCEC and NYSERDA (which have an interest in tracking local laws; I could not verify NYSERDA's local-law database before the search budget ran out). Reliability: very high for the AG outcome in Massachusetts; the agenda items themselves are explicit. Lead: the warrant article is visible weeks before the vote and 3–9 months before the AG decision.

### 2.4 Zoning changes, overlays and land values

**Chain.** Planning Board work session → draft amendment → first publication of notice (MA freeze date) → hearing → Town Meeting → AG approval → permits filed under the new rules → land trades at the new value.

**Evidence on land values.** Freemark's Chicago study found upzoned parcels sold for significantly more after the change with no increase in permitted units over five years ([Planetizen summary](https://planetizen.com/node/133031)); Anagol, Ferreira and Rexer found São Paulo's 2016 block-level upzoning raised multifamily permit filings quickly and the housing stock by 1.4–2.2%, with prices down 0.4–0.9% ([NBER](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/29440.html); [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/research/zoning-reform-economic-value)); Turner, Haughwout and van der Klaauw found large negative effects of regulation on land value at municipal borders ([Econometrica 2014](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2014/07/01/land-use-regulation-and-welfare)); an Auckland working paper argues upzoning raises the value of land-intensive properties ([Auckland EPC](https://cdn.auckland.ac.nz/assets/business/about/our-research/research-institutes-and-centres/Economic-Policy-Centre--EPC-/013WP.pdf)). For Greater Boston specifically, the Boston Fed's lot-level study of 101 municipalities finds relaxing density limits is the lever that adds multifamily supply and that 40B "is not a substitute" for it ([Boston Fed WP 22-10](https://www.bostonfed.org/publications/research-department-working-paper/2022/how-to-increase-housing-affordability-understanding-local-deterrents-to-building-multifamily-housing.aspx)). The direction of the parcel-level effect is settled; the size depends on whether redevelopment actually pencils.

**The live Massachusetts case.** Under the MBTA Communities law, 149 municipalities were fully compliant with 468 districts as of 10 April 2026 ([EOHLC atlas](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/mbta-communities-district-atlas)), over 90% were compliant or conditionally compliant by late 2025 ([Planetizen](https://www.planetizen.com/news/2025/12/136625-over-90-massachusetts-towns-have-changed-zoning-comply-mbta-communities-law)), the AG sued nine holdouts ([Bisnow](https://www.bisnow.com/news/boston/multifamily/state-sues-nine-cities-and-towns-over-mbta-communities-compliance-132993)), and the state counts at least 6,000 homes in development in the new districts ([NBC Boston](https://www.nbcboston.com/news/local/housing-mbta-law/3886679/)). Every one of those projects passes through a Planning Board agenda we read.

**Buyers and value.** Housing developers and land investors (the §6 filing window is worth eight years of frozen zoning on a subdivision); land-use attorneys; zoning-report vendors such as Zoneomics ($92–$279 a month, [pricing](https://www.zoneomics.com/pricing)) as licensees. Reliability: medium. Draft-bylaw items are often titled vaguely ("zoning bylaw amendments, discussion"), and the owner's own report records a case where a data-center moratorium discussion was not named on the agenda at all (internal). Lead: weeks to months before first notice.

### 2.5 Affordable housing (40B) and the construction chain

**Chain.** Project-eligibility letter → ZBA comprehensive-permit hearing (on our agendas, with unit counts and addresses) → decision within 40 days of closing → appeal or building permit → start about a month after permit → contractor, supplier and lender demand.

**Evidence.** Hingham issued a final comprehensive permit for 32 condominiums on 8 December 2025 ([Hingham](https://hingham-ma.gov/728/Pending-40B-Applications)); Franklin drafted a 254-unit permit in December 2025 ([Franklin](https://www.franklinma.gov/DocumentCenter/View/7947/Draft-Decision121525clean)); Brookline took 18 months from eligibility letter to draft decision ([Brookline](https://www.brooklinema.gov/1311/Chapter-40B-Status)). Greater Boston high-rise costs run about $1 million a unit and multifamily input costs are up 52.8% since February 2020, so suburban garden and mid-rise product dominates the pipeline (five high-rises completed since 2024 against 47 garden-style and 88 mid-rise) ([CRE Daily](https://www.credaily.com/?p=223405)). I could not find statewide counts of comprehensive permits issued per year before the search budget ran out; EOHLC's SHI reports would hold them.

**Buyers and value.** This is the market ConstructConnect, Dodge, Building Radar and Curate already serve: Curate was built on "meeting minutes and agendas" for construction and engineering firms ([UW–Madison](https://news.wisc.edu/uw-madison-spinoff-gives-builders-a-head-start-on-new-projects)). Prices are modest per seat (§4). Reliability: very high (40B items are explicit and carry unit counts). Lead: 6–24 months before groundbreaking.

### 2.6 Cell towers and wetlands

Cell towers: a special-permit hearing starts a 150-day clock; the tower, once built, produces a ground lease that aggregators buy out, with advisers calling offers below 10–11 times annual rent or under $150,000 "low-ball" and quoting rents from $500 to $3,000 a month ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/node/17209/printable/print); [American Tower lease acquisitions](https://americantower.com/us/ground-rooftop-lease-acquisitions)). Buyers: tower-lease buyers and landowner advisers. Low frequency, modest value.

Wetlands: the 21-day hearing and 21-day decision clocks make Notices of Intent the earliest public, dated signal that a specific parcel is about to be disturbed ([GRSM](https://www.grsm.com/insight/massachusetts-wetland-protection-act-timelines/)). Most NOIs are small; the large ones duplicate what the Planning Board agenda already shows. Buyers: contractors, wetland consultants. Low value per item.

### 2.7 Utilities, ISOs and regulators

Utilities see "five to 10 times more interconnection requests than data centers actually being built" and about 30% of surveyed utilities now derate requested load by project maturity, including "permitting progress" ([Utility Dive, 15 May 2025](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/a-fraction-of-proposed-data-centers-will-get-built-utilities-are-wising-up/748214/)). A town-by-town record of which jurisdictions bar large loads, standalone storage or ground-mount solar is a derating input National Grid, Eversource, NYISO and the New York PSC (Case 26-E-0045) do not have at town grain. Value is real but the sales cycle is a regulatory one. Lead: years.

### 2.8 Hedge funds

The only evidence that pipeline news moves traded prices is hyperscaler-scale: TD Cowen's note on Microsoft cancelling "a couple of hundred MWs" of leases sent Digital Realty down 3.4% and Vistra down 5% in a day ([Quartz](https://qz.com/microsoft-stock-data-center-lease-ai-td-cowen-bofa-1851766114); [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/307558/microsoft-stock-wobbles-after-data-center-pullback)). No town in our three states hosts load at that scale (ISO-NE: 285 MW in study). Funds spent $2.8 billion on alternative data in 2025 and Neudata puts most datasets at $25,000–$150,000 a year ([AOL/Bloomberg](https://www.aol.com/news/money-managers-hungrier-ever-obscure-140001573.html); [Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsx5x6m2pew8jzgavmyo/corner-office/how-much-are-managers-paying-for-data)), but I found no vendor selling town-level permitting signals to them and no evidence our signal would move a price. Rank last.

## 4. The ranking, explained

Scores are judgments, 1–5 each, and the product of the three is the rank. "Value" is the money the buyer can gain or avoid losing by acting on the signal, discounted by how many such buyers exist in three states. "Early" is lead time over the money event relative to what the buyer can get elsewhere. "Reliable" is how plainly today's agenda data shows the signal without minutes or outcomes.

1. **MA AG outcome for solar/BESS bylaws (3×5×5).** Value is moderate because sophisticated counsel already knows *Tracer Lane II*; what they lack is the town-by-town list, the clause-level pattern (which setbacks and caps survived in 109 of 114 bylaws) and the timing. Lead is the full AG window plus the pre-vote weeks. Reliability is the best we have: it is the one place we hold outcomes.
2. **NY BESS moratoria, extensions and expiries (5×3×4).** Highest value per buyer (six- to seven-figure withdrawal costs, 98–108 towns, 1 GW stalled) and the clearest statutory logic (towns, not ORES, decide). Lead is 1–3 months on adoption and longer on expiries. Marked down for reliability only because our New York coverage is thin.
3. **40B / 3A housing pipeline (3×4×5).** Explicit items, long lead, many buyers, but a crowded market with low per-seat prices.
4. **Data-center ledger (4×3×4).** Big dollars per project, but few projects in New England, pre-emptive bylaws in MA, and a statewide order in NY that overrides towns above 50 MW for a year. Still the clearest "nowhere else" dataset at town grain.
5. **Zoning-change filing window (4×4×3).** Large value for housing developers in Massachusetts because of §6; reliability suffers from vague agenda titles.
6–9. Opposition mapping for PR firms, utility load-derating input, wetlands/cell leads, hedge funds: real but smaller, slower or unproven.

## 6. Limits, stated plainly

- **Three states, uneven depth.** Massachusetts is deep (240 of 351 towns automated, internal). New York, where the battery moratoria and the data-center queue are, is thin (two New York municipalities in the data-center ledger, internal). Rhode Island has seven ledger rows and I found no 2025–26 Rhode Island solar moratorium coverage. Products 2 and 4 need New York coverage before they can be sold.
- **Agendas, not outcomes.** We do not yet read minutes or local-law filings, so we cannot say what share of "discussion" items became moratoria, how many moratoria were extended, or how many projects withdrew. The one outcome series we hold is the Massachusetts AG record. Before any product claims to "predict", build the outcome table: minutes, AG decisions, NY local-law filings, NYISO/ISO-NE queue withdrawals, and the per-item `first_seen` stamps the deep dive says are now being recorded (internal). Sell base rates with counts until then.
- **Agendas hide topics.** The owner's own reporting records a Spartanburg County, South Carolina resident who learned of a data-center discussion 30 minutes before a meeting because the agenda never named it, and in Massachusetts one agenda in ten posts two days or less before the meeting and 4% post on the day (internal). Our lead is real but not guaranteed on every item.
- **The state is overtaking the town.** EO 62 in New York, the Massachusetts consolidated permit and the AG's *Tracer Lane II* line all move decisions away from the bodies we read. Town agendas stay decisive for standalone storage in New York, for data centers under 50 MW, for sub-25 MW clean energy in Massachusetts (12-month local permit), and for all housing.
- **Rerouting is asserted more than measured.** The one direct statement that developers refile "in a different but nearby location" is a caveat in a critique of Data Center Watch; the academic spillover literature shows neighbours copying restrictions, not developers hopping. Do not put a rerouting claim on the site.
- **No invented numbers.** Prices for Heatmap Pro, Transect, Hamlet, Curate, Building Radar, Zonda, Dodge and PR retainers are not public and are not estimated here; ConstructConnect figures are third-party trackers; Heatmap's $260 billion sits behind a paywall; the web-search allowance ran out before I could verify site-selection consultant fees, NYSERDA's local-law database, statewide annual 40B permit counts or Sabin's Massachusetts and Rhode Island appendices. Those are the first follow-ups.
- **Hedge funds are not a market for this.** Nothing in three states moves a REIT or utility; say so if asked.
