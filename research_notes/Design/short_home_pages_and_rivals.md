# Short home pages: what the best sites do, who our neighbours are, and what Agenda Ahead should do

Prepared 2 November 2026. All pages were read on that day.

How the numbers were made. Each site was opened in headless Chromium through Playwright with the default browser identity, at 1366x900 and 390x844, with a 4 second wait after load (12 seconds for Linear, whose hero paints late). "First screen words" counts the words of visible text that fall inside the viewport before any scroll, including navigation and any cookie or donation banner that was showing. "Page words" is the whole page's visible text. "Links" is the number of `<a href>` elements. "Sections" is a hand count of distinct blocks on the page from the screenshot and the heading list. "Screens" is page height divided by viewport height at 1366x900. Screenshots are in `/tmp/claude-0/-home-user-sulmusic2-star/815cfdd0-e090-5147-aee8-a5db345f6b5d/scratchpad/design6/shots/` as `<site>-1366.png` and `<site>-390.png`. Raw numbers and the first-screen text of every page are in `/tmp/claude-0/-home-user-sulmusic2-star/815cfdd0-e090-5147-aee8-a5db345f6b5d/scratchpad/design6/data/sites-*-results.json`. Where a site served a bot check, a 403, or a human-verification page, it is marked "blocked" and was not bypassed; no user agent was changed. For a few blocked sites a plain text fetch (WebFetch) worked and is used, and is marked as such.

Scripts: `/tmp/claude-0/-home-user-sulmusic2-star/815cfdd0-e090-5147-aee8-a5db345f6b5d/scratchpad/design6/scripts/measure.js` (measurement) and `dump_aa.js` (our page's full text, saved to `data/agendaahead-text.txt`).

## Section 2. The patterns, with counts

Counts are over the 40 pages that could be read (Section 1, excluding ours and the blocked ones).

### 2.1 Two kinds of first screen

- Pitch-first (a headline, a line, one action): 25 pages. First-screen words run from 13 (Google) to 161 (Linear); the median is 68 (PlanningAlerts). Only five go above 120: Stripe 124, Linear 161, theSkimm 199, Pinboard 201, Basecamp 597, and the last two are deliberately plain prose.
- Content-first (the first screen is the content): 15 pages (Hacker News, Wikipedia, Craigslist, Substack, Weather.gov, Airbnb, Boston.gov, BBC, NPR, text.npr, Guardian, OpenCouncil, The Mill, Block Club, Outlier). First-screen words run 64 to 653, median 176, but the words are items, not explanation.

Our first screen is pitch-first at 129 words, almost double the pitch-first median, and it is the only pitch-first page in the set that puts a paragraph, an instruction line, a form, three example links and a captioned map all on one screen.

### 2.2 How many words, how many blocks, how long

- Whole page: median about 700 words across the 40 (range 12 to 3,366). Ours is 609, a little under the median. By word count we are ordinary, not overloaded.
- Links: median 108. Ours is 110. But 58 of ours are map-dot links to towns inside the hero, which is a lot of links hidden in one picture.
- Blocks: median 6. The pages people name as short and to the point (Google, Hacker News, Craigslist, Pinboard, Councilmatic, Weather.gov, text.npr, OpenCouncil, Nextdoor, Airbnb) have 1 to 3 blocks. Ours has 8 before the footer.
- Height: 9 of 40 are 1.6 screens or less; 7 are 2 to 5 screens; 15 are 5 to 10; 9 are over 10. Ours is 3.5 on desktop and 5.9 on a phone, which is the short end.

So the complaint "way overloaded" is not about length. It is about the first screen doing seven things and the page having eight kinds of block, several of which (how it works, who makes this, three disclaimers, a second lookup form, a week strip, a subject grid) the best pages keep off the home page entirely.

### 2.3 What the best pages leave off (counts of 40)

- A "how it works" block on the home page: 8 have one (PlanningAlerts, Patch, Councilmatic, Documenters, Kagi, DuckDuckGo, Basecamp, Hey); 32 do not. Every content-first page leaves it off.
- A "who makes this" paragraph: 3 (Documenters, Patch, Pinboard). Seven more say it in one line: Wikipedia ("hosted by the Wikimedia Foundation"), PlanningAlerts ("An OpenAustralia Foundation service"), Kagi ("100% user funded. 0% advertising."), Documenters ("powered by City Bureau"), and the official-site banners on GOV.UK, USA.gov and Boston.gov. The pattern is one line of provenance, not a paragraph.
- Disclaimers on the home page: 0 of 40, apart from the three government "official website" banners. Ours has three disclaimer sentences ("Free, with no account to make", "A program writes each sentence...", "Not legal advice. Confirm dates with the town clerk.").
- The main action repeated at the bottom: 8 of 40, all pages over 5 screens (Stripe, Notion, Kagi, Hey, DuckDuckGo, Duolingo, Shovels, Morning Brew). None of the nine one-screen pages repeats anything. Ours repeats the town box at 3.5 screens.
- A map: 2 of 40, and both are the content (Weather.gov's alert map; the craigslist.org world directory). The craigslist city page people actually use has no map. Ours is decorative: dots that say nothing a line of text could not say.
- Example links beside a box: 2 (Kagi's three example searches, Craigslist's nearby cities). Ours has three ("Or try Carver, Amherst or Clay, NY"), which is fine and matches Kagi.

### 2.4 The hook types that work, with counts

1. The thing itself (today's content or live data on the first screen): 15 of 40. Hacker News, Wikipedia, Craigslist, Substack, Weather.gov, Airbnb, Boston.gov, BBC, NPR, text.npr, Guardian, OpenCouncil, The Mill, Block Club, Outlier.
2. A box to type into (search, address, town, ZIP): 10 of 40. Google, Kagi, DuckDuckGo, GOV.UK, USA.gov, Weather.gov, Airbnb, Wikipedia, PlanningAlerts, Patch. (Three overlap with type 1.)
3. A promise headline and one button: 17 of 40. Notion, Linear, Stripe, Hey, Duolingo, Robinhood, Citizen, Shovels, Apple, Nextdoor, Documenters, Councilmatic, Morning Brew, theSkimm, 6AM City, Basecamp, Pinboard.
4. A live sample or number inside the pitch: 6 of 40. Stripe's GDP counter, Basecamp's "177,188 people working right now", Morning Brew's "Latest newsletter Nov 2" card, Hey's review quotes, Linear's and Notion's product screenshots. Among neighbours (Section 3) The Common News and GatherGov put a live sample next to the headline.
5. A question: 1 of 40 (PlanningAlerts: "What is being built and knocked down in your area? Find out here, for free.").
6. An invitation to join or sign up: 6 of 40. Nextdoor, Patch, Morning Brew, theSkimm, Substack, Documenters.

Which fit an information service with no accounts and no price: types 1 and 2, alone or together. Every information service in the set (Hacker News, Wikipedia, Craigslist, Weather.gov, GOV.UK, USA.gov, text.npr, Boston.gov, OpenCouncil, PlanningAlerts) leads with the content, a box, or both. None leads with a promise-and-button alone; that pattern belongs to products that need a sign-up. Type 5 fits too, as a wording for a type-2 page, but only one site in the set uses it.

### 2.5 Phone width

On a 390 pixel screen the pitch-first pages drop to a median of 61 first-screen words (Google 13, Duolingo 17, Airbnb 14, Notion 49). Ours shows 90. Hacker News still shows 15 stories on a phone's first screen (hackernews-390.png). GOV.UK shows the headline and the search box and nothing else once the cookie banner is dismissed (govuk-390.png). Our phone first screen holds the headline, the 40-word paragraph, the instruction, the form and the example towns, and the map begins below (agendaahead-390.png).

### 4.3 Three options for the single hook, with the evidence

Option A. The thing itself. The first screen is the headline, one line, the town box, and the five biggest stories start at once.

Risk: on a phone the stories push the box down; keep the box above the stories.

Option B. The lookup. The first screen is the headline, one line and the town box, nothing else; stories come on the next screen.
Evidence: 10 of 40 lead with a box, and all the government and reference sites do (Google 13 words, GOV.UK 80 words with the box as the only action, USA.gov, Weather.gov's "City, St or ZIP", Wikipedia, Kagi with three example searches). Among neighbours, Patch (town box, https://patch.com), SeeGov (ZIP box, https://seegov.org), Aware (town search strip, https://www.aware-insights.net), SimpleCity (city picker in the header, https://simplecity.app) and PlanningAlerts (address box, https://www.planningalerts.org.au) all do it. Risk: a visitor who does not know what we do must type before seeing anything; Nextdoor and Patch accept that because the value is personal, and ours is too (your town).

Option C. The question. Headline: "What is your town about to decide?" then "Find out every morning, free." then the box.
Evidence: PlanningAlerts has run on "What is being built and knocked down in your area? Find out here, for free." plus an address box for years, funded by supporters at $20 a month (https://www.planningalerts.org.au). The Common News names the pain in its headline ("before it disappears into a PDF"), Hey leads with a claim. Only 1 of 40 pages uses a question, so this is the weakest evidence, but the question is literally what the site answers each morning and it reads well in seven words on a phone.

Recommendation: A for the content and B for the action, with C's wording if the owner prefers a question. That is: headline, one line, one box, then the five stories, then one subject line, then the footer. Weather.gov (box plus today's headline on one screen) and Patch's first screen (one sentence, one line, a town box) are the two pages that already do exactly this.
