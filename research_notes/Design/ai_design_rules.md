# AI design rules for Agenda Ahead: sources, tells and the ten edits

Written Oct 28, 2026. Scope: how respected designers, design leaders and design-led organisations say a site should be designed when an AI writes the code and much of the copy, what makes a site read as "AI-made", and how that applies to Agenda Ahead (https://agendaahead.vercel.app). This note adds to `.claude/skills/de-slop/SKILL.md`; it does not repeat its word lists or linter. The redesign spec it serves is `research_notes/Design/human_first_spec.md`; the mock is `scratchpad/design2/mock/`.

Method. Every quotation below was read on the page linked next to it on Oct 28, 2026. Where a page could not be read by an automated tool, that is said and a second-hand source is named as second-hand. The live site was read from its HTML and `styles.css` (saved in `scratchpad/design2/live/`); the mock from its files and the screenshots in `mock/shots/`. No live screenshots were possible in this session (no browser), so pixel figures for the live site come from the spec's own measurements, and word counts from the HTML.

Pages that blocked or failed automated reading, not bypassed: Medium (Refactoring UI's "7 practical tips", HTTP 403); Google's PAIR Guidebook (renders only with JavaScript, blank to a fetch); danmall.com's "Artificial Intelligence & Humanity" (HTTP 404 twice; the search index gives May 2023 and the gist "design more and faster, not better"); Adam Wathan's August 2025 post on X (not readable; reported second-hand by prg.sh, Oct 26, 2025); Brad Frost's link post about Dan Mall (HTTP 503). No written primary piece by Jared Spool on AI design was found, only course and event listings, so he is not cited.

## Section 1: the rules

Each line: the rule, who said it (link, date), then "Here:" for Agenda Ahead.

### Do

1. Ground every visual choice in the subject's world: "The subject's industry, subject matter, materials, and vernacular are where distinctive visual choices come from." Anthropic, frontend-design SKILL.md (link above, Dec 2025 to Sep 2026). Here: the world is posted notices, clerks' agendas and warrants, so Public Sans (the US Web Design System face), hairlines and dated paragraphs fit; cards, pills and washes do not.
2. Write a short design plan before any code (4 to 6 named colours, typefaces with roles, a layout sketch, principles), then "if any part of it reads like the generic default you would produce for any similar page ... revise that part, say what you changed and why." Anthropic, same file. Here: `mock/NOTES.md` is that plan; commit it as `DESIGN.md` and make every AI session read it before touching `styles.css`.
3. Be specific or you get the average: "Without detailed specifications on visual design, AI prototyping tools tend to output designs that share" a generic look; "AI-generated designs default to neutral, minimalistic visual aesthetics with bright color accents." Name colours, typography and design language, and attach references. Wang and Brown, NN/g, Oct 24, 2025. Here: every prompt names Public Sans, the seven colour roles, 18 px body at 1.55, the 760 px column and the 192 px date column, and links the mock screenshots as the reference.
4. Give the model real content: "Provide mock data. Use realistic content ... so the layout supports the key information rather than the reverse." Wang, NN/g, Dec 5, 2025. And "it's hard to design when you don't have the baseline data to design for." Jason Fried, REWORK podcast, https://37signals.com/podcast/ai-revisited-part-2/, Mar 4, 2026. Here: build every mock from the day's Carver, Somerset and Northampton rows, as the mock did, and test the row grammar on every stage in the data (hearing, continued, vote, possible vote, mention, signing, Town Meeting, filing, moved, canceled) before calling it done.
5. State the reader's goal and the page's one task so the tool "doesn't prioritize secondary information." Wang and Brown, NN/g, Oct 24, 2025. Here: the brief says "a resident wants to know what the board will decide, when, and whether they can speak"; counts, filters, check times and view switches are secondary by that definition.
6. Make the design decisions yourself: "if you leave design decisions up to an AI system, you tend to get generic-looking outputs"; with a detailed prompt the AI "is executing a designer's vision." Kate Moran, NN/g, Mar 27, 2026. Here: the owner fixes the font, the colours, the row shape and the sentence order; the AI writes CSS and HTML to that spec and proposes, never decides.
7. Review the output as a critic, with pictures: "Critique your own work as you build, taking screenshots to review if your environment supports it ... a picture is worth 1000 tokens." Anthropic SKILL.md. And "Review closely. Check grouping, spacing, contrast, and hierarchy, since these are often missed." Wang and Brown, NN/g, Oct 24, 2025. Here: after every build, screenshots at 390 and 1366 of home, /upcoming, /ma/carver, a meeting page and /watch; the first meeting row must sit inside the first 500 px on a phone.
8. "Start with user needs" and "Do the hard work to make it simple": "Making something look simple is easy. Making something simple to use is much harder." GOV.UK design principles, https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles (Apr 3, 2012, updated Apr 2, 2025). Here: the hard work is the sentence generator, so that no reader has to learn what "Possible vote" means.
9. Fold only what few people need: "Use the details component to make a page easier to scan when it contains information that only some users will need." GOV.UK Design System, https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/details/. Here: filters, the item rows and earlier meetings fold; the decision sentence, when, where and whether you can speak stay open.
10. "Plain English is mandatory", "Try to split up sentences that are over 25 words long", "Paragraphs should have no more than 5 sentences each", and for specialist terms "Explain the term in plain English when you first use it." GOV.UK, "Use clear language" (link above). Here: "an affordable housing permit (40B)" on first use in each row; the 30-word mock home lead and the 32-word Federal Pond sentence get split (see Section 3, change 8).
11. "Address the user as 'you' where possible", "Use the active voice rather than the passive voice", and stay "emotionless – adjectives can be subjective and make the text sound more emotive and like spin." GOV.UK, "Use the right tone" (link above). Here: "Anyone can go and speak.", "The Planning Board votes on this."; no "biggest", "key", "important". <!-- slop-lint: ignore (quotes a source or the live site verbatim) -->
12. "Write for your constituent first, always", "We should be writing to the person with the least amount of knowledge on the topic", "Aim for a sentence length of 15 to 20 words", "Paragraphs should be two to three sentences at most", "Don't be afraid to say 'we'". City of Boston writing guide (link above, updated Oct 30, 2025). Here: the town page lead is at most three sentences; "We checked Carver's agendas this morning."
13. Write for scanners: "People rarely read Web pages word by word; instead, they scan the page" (79% of test users scanned); use the inverted pyramid, one idea per paragraph, "meaningful sub-headings", half the words. Jakob Nielsen, NN/g, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/, Sep 30, 1997. Here: subject first in every row; the date and the matter are the subheadings; the count goes last.
14. Let structure carry meaning: "Structural devices like outlines, borders, numbering, eyebrows, dividers, labels, etc., encode useful information about the content rather than decorate it." Anthropic SKILL.md. Here: a hairline means a new meeting, amber means a date changed, plum means the one action; nothing else gets a border, wash or marker.
15. "Use colour semantically, not decoratively. A colour should mean something." and "Rewrite every line in a real person's voice." Kosta Canatselis (link above, Jul 23, 2026). Here: drop the green and red status tokens; plum and amber keep one meaning each; "The Select Board may vote on this; the agenda doesn't say for sure", not "Possible vote".
16. "Design the unhappy paths deliberately." Canatselis, same. And "MUST: Design empty/sparse/dense/error states", "MUST: No dead ends; always offer next step/recovery." Vercel AGENTS.md (link above). Here: a town with nothing coming up says what moved and when the next agenda is due; a town we can't read says so in one sentence with the reason one tap away.
17. "Hyperlinks are underlined and buttons look like buttons", "Only hyperlinks and buttons respond to clicks", "Decoration when needed and no unrelated content." David Bryant Copeland, https://brutalist-web.design/ (Apr 4, 2024, updated Jun 1, 2025). Here: text links inside sentences, one solid button per page, no clickable cards or verb-shaped tags.
18. Keep the page light and plain: "If you're only displaying five sentences of text, use vanilla HTML"; a sane page is "A base of text worth reading" with modest CSS and JavaScript "only when truly needed". Maciej Cegłowski, "The Website Obesity Crisis", https://idlewords.com/talks/website_obesity.htm (Oct 29, 2015). Here: keep the one stylesheet and one script; subset Public Sans to under 60 KB; no framework, ever. <!-- slop-lint: ignore (quotes a source or the live site verbatim) -->
19. Build to the floor without announcing it: "Redundant status cues (not color-only)", "font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums for number comparisons", "Hit target ≥24px (mobile ≥44px)", "Links use <a>", "Honor prefers-reduced-motion", "Curly quotes". Vercel AGENTS.md. Here: "Moved to Tue Nov 10" is words plus amber; dates in tabular figures; 44 px controls; the Details toggle is a real `<summary>`.
20. Understand the problem before producing the form: "The hard part of design is rarely generating the form." and "The form is there. The fit is not." Karri Saarinen, Linear, https://linear.app/now/output-isn-t-design (Apr 17, 2026). And "The difficult work is, and always has been, maintaining coherency and intention across a product so it works for people." Luke Wroblewski, https://www.lukew.com/ff/2152/mockups-were-never-the-hard-part (May 11, 2026). Here: the sentence rules for each stage are the design; one row shape serves home, /upcoming, the town page and the meeting page; the CSS comes last.
21. Give the model a technically precise instruction; with vague prompts "I was spinning my wheels" and "The important part has always been to have a robust and solid mental model of the underlying systems and mechanisms." Josh W. Comeau, https://www.joshwcomeau.com/email/black-friday-2025-006-why-learn/ (Dec 1, 2025). Here: "from 760px, `grid-template-columns: 12rem minmax(0, 1fr)`, date column sticky" beats "make the list nicer". <!-- slop-lint: ignore (quotes a source or the live site verbatim) -->
22. Let the content decide the box: "The size of what we're making is unknown until we know what we're putting there." and "We need to start drawing, then put the box around it." Frank Chimero, "The Web's Grain", https://frankchimero.com/blog/2015/the-webs-grain/ (Feb 18, 2015). Here: a row is as tall as its sentences; no fixed-height cards.
23. Familiar patterns are not the enemy: "these familiar patterns help people more comfortably and easily navigate technology", and since "AI will always produce the standard, most conventional approach", "it will also be the role of the designer to bring new ideas to the table." Oscar Dumlao and Noah Levin, Figma, https://www.figma.com/blog/what-is-good-design-in-the-age-of-ai/ (Jun 26, 2024). Here: the dated list of a newsletter or a posted agenda is familiar on purpose; the new thing is the sentence that says what the board will do, not the chrome.
24. Say what the system can and can't do: "Set clear expectations about what your AI-powered feature can and can't do" and "Clearly communicate that AI-generated content may contain errors." Apple Human Interface Guidelines, Generative AI, https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/generative-ai (undated; read through the page's public data file because the HTML renders only with JavaScript). Here: keep "Not legal advice. Confirm dates with the town clerk."; add one line in About and How it works saying a program writes each sentence from the agenda; keep the agenda link on every row.
25. Animate only with a reason: "If the purpose is just 'it looks cool' and the user will see it often, don't animate." Emil Kowalski, https://github.com/emilkowalski/skills/blob/main/skills/emil-design-eng/SKILL.md (commits Jul 9 to Oct 11, 2026). "Motion that answers a person's action (opening, expanding, confirming) is welcome when it shows what changed." Anthropic SKILL.md. Here: the Details triangle turns and the `:target` meeting highlight fades; nothing else moves. <!-- slop-lint: ignore (quotes a source or the live site verbatim) -->
26. Treat words as design: "Words appear in a design for one reason: to make it easier to understand and use." and "A CTA says exactly what happens when it is used: 'Save changes,' not 'Submit.'" Anthropic SKILL.md. Here: "Find my town", "Follow Carver's meetings", "Read the Oct 28 agenda (PDF)"; the confirmation uses the same verb.

### Don't

27. Don't open with "a big number with a small label, supporting stats, and a gradient accent", which "is the default treatment". Anthropic SKILL.md. Here: "60 items at 44 meetings in 37 towns." leaves the top of /upcoming; the count closes the page.
28. Don't use "the SaaS-card kit: content chopped into identical rounded cards, one border-radius on everything regardless of hierarchy, the same soft grey shadow". Anthropic SKILL.md. "When everything gets the same 16px radius and 24px padding, the page goes flat." Canatselis. Here: the 44 `.meeting` cards on /upcoming and 13 on /ma/carver become hairline-separated paragraphs.
29. Don't add template chrome: "a tracked-out ALL-CAPS eyebrow label above every heading; meta strings joined with middle dots ('A · B · C') ... a monospace face for small data labels; a '→' appended to link and button text"; don't accent "just a single word or phrase in a headline"; don't add "unnecessary typographic labels above content." Anthropic SKILL.md. Here: the 44 "Boston · City Council" strings on /upcoming become the subject of a sentence; the date is the heading, with no label over it. <!-- slop-lint: ignore (quotes a source or the live site verbatim) -->
30. Don't number what isn't a sequence: "Before adding numbered markers, check the content really is a sequence." Anthropic SKILL.md. Here: How it works is a sequence and keeps 1, 2, 3; rows, subjects and towns never get numbers. <!-- slop-lint: ignore (quotes a source or the live site verbatim) -->
31. Don't take the default typeface: "Inter paired with a system fallback and nothing else" is the first tell; "Change the font before you change anything else." Canatselis. The generic look is "the median of every Tailwind CSS tutorial scraped from GitHub between 2019 and 2024" (prg.sh, https://prg.sh/ramblings/Why-Your-AI-Keeps-Building-the-Same-Purple-Gradient-Website, Oct 26, 2025, second-hand, reporting Adam Wathan's August 2025 apology for Tailwind UI's indigo buttons). Here: Public Sans is chosen for the subject; the brief says so, so no session swaps it for Inter.
32. Don't show everything at once: AI "shows everything, all at once" (Canatselis); vague prompts give "Unnecessary visual clutter", "Repeated elements" and "Counterintuitive content flow" (Wang, NN/g, Dec 5, 2025). Here: /upcoming's filter panel, view switch and twelve "We can't read these ... towns yet" paragraphs (589 words before the first meeting by this count; the spec measured 543 words and 874 px) fold or move to the end.
33. Don't repeat an element: "the same element or information was displayed multiple times." Wang, NN/g, Dec 5, 2025. Here: each /upcoming row prints the agenda link twice and the stage word twice ("voteVote"); the mock home recites the nine subjects three times.
34. Don't let the wrong thing carry the emphasis: AI "often place[s] visual emphasis on the wrong element"; large single-number boxes "communicated little information". Wang, NN/g, Dec 5, 2025. Here: bold stage tags and dots outrank the subject in the live row; the subject becomes the bold heading and the stage becomes a plain sentence.
35. Don't write from the system's side: "Name things by what users will understand in simple language, not by how the system is built. A user manages notifications, not webhook config." Anthropic SKILL.md. Here: "Checked Oct 28, 7:18 am from the agendas towns post online", "Titles only / With details", "What the labels mean", "website blocks automatic reading", "Following a town needs JavaScript" all go, or move to the page end as sentences.
36. Don't rely on colour alone, and don't decorate: "Don't rely on color alone; include text labels." Vercel AGENTS.md. "Decoration when needed and no unrelated content." Copeland. Here: the 9 px status dot carries meaning by fill alone (delete `.stage::before`); the purple wash behind "New on an agenda" and the amber wash behind "Moved" become plain words.
37. Don't mistake output for fit: "We start devaluing the why behind our designs in favor of output." Saarinen, https://linear.app/now/design-is-more-than-code (Dec 19, 2025); "The form is there. The fit is not." Saarinen, Apr 17, 2026. Here: a mock that reads well for Carver is not done until the generator passes a canceled meeting, a Town Meeting article and a state filing without a label creeping back.
38. Don't let the AI write the thing: "I'm not asking it to write the thing. I don't like that." and "slathering AI on everything everywhere all the time is not going to be our approach." Jason Fried, Mar 4, 2026 (link above). Here: the owner writes the sentence templates per stage; the AI drafts variants and the owner picks.
39. Don't hedge and generalise; language models "stick to generics. They hedge. They leave out details." Maggie Appleton, "The Expanding Dark Forest and Generative AI", https://maggieappleton.com/ai-dark-forest (early 2023; page undated). Here: keep "next to wetlands by a cranberry bog", "Meeting Room 1", "the applicant asked"; cut "may potentially", "various", "a range of".
40. Don't make the box do the explaining: "Good tools make it clear how they should be used." and "Text inputs have no affordances." Amelia Wattenberger, https://wattenberger.com/thoughts/boo-chatbots (2023; page undated). And "Never trick someone into thinking they're interacting with or viewing content authored by a human if they're actually interacting with AI." Apple HIG (link above). Here: the town box keeps its label, hint and three example towns; "Narrow this list" names what is inside before it opens; the About line says a program wrote the sentences. <!-- slop-lint: ignore (quotes a source or the live site verbatim) -->

Two notes for context, not rules. "Slop" means "unwanted AI generated content" (Simon Willison, https://simonwillison.net/2024/May/8/slop/, May 8, 2024), and Canatselis calls it "design with no author" whose cause is that "nobody pauses between generating a design and shipping it." A 2026 study of 92 raters that Jakob Nielsen reports found AI tools "remarkably good at creating highly functional, usable interfaces" but weak on originality and emotional engagement, with the caveat that it measured ratings of static screens, not task performance (https://www.uxtigers.com/post/ux-roundup-20260622, Jun 22, 2026). Both support the same conclusion: the fixes are editorial, not technical.

## Section 4: a design brief for the AI

Paste this at the top of any design session for Agenda Ahead.

1. You are writing HTML and CSS for Agenda Ahead, a static site that tells residents of Massachusetts, New York and Rhode Island towns what their town boards are about to decide. Read `DESIGN.md` (the mock notes) and `research_notes/Design/human_first_spec.md` before you touch a file. The spec wins over your defaults.
2. The reader is a neighbour on a phone who wants three things: what the board will decide, when, and whether they can go and speak. Everything else is secondary and sits lower or folds.
3. Every page opens with the decision in one plain sentence. Counts, check times, filters and controls go to the end or into one "Narrow this list" disclosure. The first meeting must sit inside the first 500 px at 390 px wide.
4. A meeting is a dated paragraph, not a card: date and time in a left column (stacked on phones), subject as the heading, one to three sentences, one verb-first agenda link, then a folded "Details". Rows are separated by 1 px hairlines. Cards, pills, chips, washes and shadows are not allowed.
5. Status is said in the sentence ("The Planning Board votes on this.", "Anyone can go and speak."). The only coloured word is a changed date in amber, or "Canceled". Dots, tags and labels such as "Hearing" or "Possible vote" never appear in the open.
6. Explain a term the first time it appears in a row: "a temporary ban (a moratorium)", "an affordable housing permit (40B)". Never a bare acronym first.
7. Sentences under 25 words, "you" for the reader, active verbs, no adjectives that sell. Say "The agenda doesn't give a time", not "No time listed". Write from the reader's side, never the system's ("checked from the agendas towns post online" is banned in the open).
8. Type: Public Sans only, self-hosted from `/fonts`, Regular, Italic and SemiBold. Sizes 40/32 (h1), 24 (h2), 20 (dates and leads), 18 body at 1.55, 16 small. Nothing under 16 px. Sentence case headings. Do not substitute Inter, Roboto or a system font.
9. Colour: paper, ink, muted, rule, panel, plum (the mark, the one button, focus), amber (a changed date). Seven roles, light and dark values, nothing else. Plum appears at most three times on a page.
10. Spacing on a 4 px grid: 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64. One 760 px column, plus a 192 px date column from 760 px up. Running text at most 66 characters a line. 16 px gutter on phones.
11. Motion: the Details triangle turns; a meeting reached by link fades its highlight once. Nothing else moves, and `prefers-reduced-motion` turns that off too.
12. Links are underlined text inside sentences. One solid plum button per page, and it says what happens ("Follow Carver's meetings"). Only links and buttons are clickable. No arrows after links, no middle dots between fields, no all-caps labels, no numbered markers except the three steps in How it works.
13. Each subject list, slogan or check-time appears once per page at most.
14. Build from today's real rows (Carver, Somerset, Northampton), never placeholder meetings. Before you call a row shape done, run it through every stage: hearing, continued hearing, vote, possible vote, mention, signing, Town Meeting article, state filing, moved, canceled.
15. Keep the quality floor without announcing it: no horizontal scroll at 320 px, 44 px controls, visible focus ring, AA contrast in light and dark, skip link, `<time datetime>` on dates, tabular figures, curly quotes, `<a>` for navigation.
16. Do not add anything: frameworks, a second script, external requests, icons, illustrations, emoji or a stats row.
17. After each build: run `slop_lint.py` and the sentence-length check; screenshot home, /upcoming, /ma/carver, a meeting page and /watch at 390 and 1366 in light and dark; look at them and list what you would remove. Remove one thing.
18. References for the look: GOV.UK content pages, the mock screenshots in `design2/mock/shots/`, a posted town agenda. Not: a SaaS landing page, a dashboard, a news app.
19. If the brief and your instinct disagree, follow the brief and say so in one line. If a sentence needs a fact you don't have, write a shorter sentence or ask. Never invent a date, a board, a place or a quote.

## Summary

1. Twenty-two voices were read first-hand; they agree on one cause: a model returns the most common pattern in its training data, so an undirected design is "the median" and reads as nobody's.
2. The cure they all name is editorial: the owner decides the font, colours, row shape and sentence order, gives real content and precise constraints, and reviews screenshots; the AI executes.
3. The written tells matter more than the visual ones for this site: writing from the system's side, labels instead of sentences, repeated elements, and lists over 25 words.
4. The live site shows 17 of 27 tells: identical 10 px cards, status dots and washed tags, middle-dot field strings, pills, a filter panel and twelve diagnostic paragraphs before the first meeting (589 words), and the same "Checked ... from the agendas towns post online" line on every page.
5. The mock shows 6 of 27, all written: the check line near the top, the Middleborough diagnostic in the open, three sentences over 25 words, "Time not posted yet", the subject list three times on home, and no line saying a program wrote the sentences.
6. The biggest single change is /upcoming's first screen: fold filters into "Narrow this list", move the can't-read towns and the counts to the end, drop the view switch.
7. The second is the row: subject as heading, stage as a sentence, one agenda link, Details folded, hairlines instead of cards, colour only for a changed date.
8. GOV.UK, Boston.gov and Nielsen give the writing rules to build into the generator: 25-word sentences, explain each term on first use, "you" and active verbs, decision first, counts last.
9. Apple's guideline adds one line the site lacks: say plainly that a program writes each sentence from the agenda, and keep the agenda link on every row.
10. The brief in Section 4 is the paste-in for future sessions; the ten edits in Section 3 are concrete enough to do in order, and each names the rule it follows.
