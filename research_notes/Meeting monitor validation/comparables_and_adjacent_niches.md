# Comparables and adjacent niches: businesses that monitor government activity and sell alerts or data

Research date: 2026-10-10. About 30 web searches plus direct page fetches. Many vendor pricing pages returned 403/405 errors or require a demo, so "not published" usually means the vendor only quotes on request.

**Labels used in the tables**
- **[V]**: verified this session from a primary source (company page, SEC filing, press release) or named trade press.
- **[R]**: reported only by a third-party aggregator (Caplight, Latka, CB Insights, Sacra and similar). Treat as an estimate.
- **[K]**: background knowledge that was not re-checked this session. Confirm before relying on it.
- No price appears anywhere unless a source stated it.

## What this means

The model has been proven several times over. Monitoring government records and selling the alerts is an established business, and buyers have paid real money for it: Onvia for $70M, Glenigan for £72.9M, Periscope for $207M, and Docket Alarm as part of the roughly $1B vLex deal.

Pricing tends to follow buyer value rather than data cost:
- **Per-jurisdiction self-serve plans of about $600–$5,000 per year** for legislation trackers (BillTrack50, Plural).
- **About $40–$100 per month** for docket alerts (Docket Alarm).
- **About $600–$1,000 per month** for permit and decision data (Shovels).
- **Enterprise contracts quoted only after a demo** whenever buyers are developers, investors or sales teams (Hamlet, Heatmap Pro, Paces, Starbridge, GovSpend).

Turning **local meetings** into data is the newest and fastest-moving part of the category:
- Hamlet's first paying customers were real-estate and data-center developers, not cities.
- Heatmap Pro charges energy developers for county-level opposition and moratorium data that it gathers by phoning every county.
- Starbridge and GovSpend mine board minutes for sales signals.
- Shovels bought ReZone in Jan 2026 to add zoning decisions from local meetings to its permit data.

That gives the Massachusetts town-meeting monitor a clear buyer (renewable, data-center, housing and real-estate developers and their land agents) and a clear exit path (permit and land-data platforms, public-sector sales-intelligence firms, and policy-tracking incumbents).

The risks show up in the same evidence:
- Free public benchmarks exist (Sabin Center, CourtListener, Councilmatic, MNPA notices), so the paid value has to come from speed, completeness and analysis, not raw access.
- FiscalNote's falling revenue and net revenue retention below 100% show that broad policy-tracking subscriptions can churn.
- The pace of new anti-renewable restrictions has slowed (165 in 2023, 70 in 2025), so the pitch should cover all land-use and siting decisions, not only bans.
- No vendor found focuses on New England town meetings, conservation commissions and planning boards. That is a gap a niche player could fill, though the big platforms may move into it.
