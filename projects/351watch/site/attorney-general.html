What a town can do

A Massachusetts town can set rules for solar and battery storage, but pausing or banning them almost never gets past the state. It can pause or ban data centers. The state’s Attorney General reviews every town bylaw before it takes effect; here is how those reviews have gone.

Checked Oct 10, 2026 from the Attorney General’s decision letters and the towns’ postings.

  • A moratorium on solar or battery storageDisapproved every time since June 2022: 15 of 15, in 14 towns.See them
  • Zoning rules for solar or battery storageApproved 105 times out of 107: 73 in full and 32 with some parts struck.See them

Why

The Attorney General reviews every town bylaw (G.L. c. 40, § 32); one the office doesn’t act on in time counts as approved. Once a zoning bylaw is approved and posted, it takes effect as of the Town Meeting vote (G.L. c. 40A, § 5). City ordinances don’t go to the Attorney General. State zoning law (G.L. c. 40A, § 3, ninth paragraph) says no zoning bylaw or ordinance may prohibit or unreasonably regulate solar energy systems, or the structures that facilitate collecting solar energy, except where necessary to protect public health, safety or welfare. It binds cities as well as towns. Since Tracer Lane II, the Attorney General judges what a bylaw is for against how much it burdens solar, and many approvals warn that using a bylaw to deny a project would run a serious risk of violating § 3.

Battery storage has that protection only through Land Court rulings that read a battery as a structure that facilitates collecting solar energy, and the Land Court is a trial court, so for batteries this isn’t settled law the way it is for solar. The rulings are NextSun Energy LLC v. Fernandes (Norton, May 9, 2023), Duxbury Energy Storage, LLC v. Town of Duxbury Zoning Board of Appeals (June 23, 2025) and Town of Wendell v. Campbell (March 12, 2026), which upheld the Attorney General’s disapproval of a licensing bylaw for battery storage; all three follow the Supreme Judicial Court’s reasoning in Tracer Lane II Realty, LLC v. City of Waltham (2022).

The Attorney General approved data-center moratoriums in Shutesbury, Templeton and Gill, because state law doesn’t protect data centers the way it protects solar; the letters are in the rulings list. Missing or out of date? Send a correction.

Words on this page

Tracer Lane II: the 2022 ruling by the state’s highest court that a town can’t use zoning to ban solar. G.L. c. 40A, § 3: the state zoning law’s solar clause; a town can’t prohibit solar or restrict it unreasonably. 225 CMR 29.00: the state’s rules, final in February 2026, for permitting small clean-energy projects with one consolidated local permit. Land Court: a state trial court for land disputes; its rulings carry less weight than the highest court’s.
